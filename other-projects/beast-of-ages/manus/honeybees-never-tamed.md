There are about a hundred million beehives on Earth. Wooden boxes, stacked in rows, painted white, owned by someone. Every one of them is full of wild animals. We have been taking honey and wax from honeybees for at least nine thousand years, longer than we have had horses. In all that time, the bee inside the box has never once become ours. The reason comes down to a flight, some thirty meters above the box, that no beekeeper gets a say in.

Start with what "ours" would even mean. Melinda Zeder is an archaeologist at the Smithsonian who has spent her career on this question. Her answer is that domestication is a relationship that runs across many generations, in which one species gains a large say over another species' breeding and care, and both sides get something out of it. The key word is breeding. A dog is a wolf whose ancestors bred beside us, litter after litter, until the animal changed. A cow is a wild ox called the aurochs that we did the same thing to. To see what that looks like in an insect, look at the silkworm. Humans have bred it for around five thousand years, and the adult moth can no longer fly. Its wings are too weak to lift it, and outside a silk farm it does not survive. A 2025 study in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society B measured how much weaker its wings have become. That is a domesticated insect. Now hold it next to the honeybee, an animal we have worked with almost twice as long.

The receipts for those nine thousand years are chemical. In 2015, a team led by Mélanie Roffet-Salque checked thousands of pieces of prehistoric pottery for the fingerprint of beeswax and published what they found in the journal Nature. The oldest traces came from Anatolia, in what is now Turkey, close to the dawn of farming in that part of the world. The first known picture of a person and a bee is about as old. It is a figure on a rope ladder on a cave wall near Bicorp, in eastern Spain, reaching into a hole with a bag while bees swarm around its head. By the pharaohs it was an industry, shown in a stone carving from around 2450 BC. And in the Jordan Valley, in what is now Israel, archaeologists digging at a town called Tel Rehov found something stranger. Inside the town, dating to around 900 BC, sat a bee yard of at least seventy-five clay hives shaped like tubes, each about eighty centimeters long, enough to produce several hundred kilograms of honey a year. The bees preserved inside those hives were the surprise. Guy Bloch and his colleagues, writing in 2010 in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, identified them as Anatolian bees, a gentler and more productive kind than the local bee of that region. Three thousand years ago, someone had shipped in better-tempered bees from hundreds of kilometers away, most likely because the local ones were too fierce. As far as anyone knows, that is the oldest evidence of trying to improve the bee. It is also the first sign of the problem.

The problem is the silkworm's, turned inside out: the bee's wings work too well. A honeybee queen mates in the open air. When she is about a week old she flies out, alone, and climbs to a patch of open sky ten to forty meters up, where male bees, called drones, from hundreds of different colonies are already circling. Over one or a few flights she mates there, in the air, with somewhere between ten and twenty drones. Each drone that succeeds dies a moment later; part of him stays behind, and the next drone clears it away. Then she flies home carrying enough sperm for the rest of her life, and after that she never mates again. Bring in gentle bees from far away, as the keepers at Tel Rehov did, and within a generation or two the local males have bred the gentleness right back out.

Domestication runs on control of breeding, and breeding is exactly what the bee does out of reach. Think about what that means for the person who owns the box. A horse breeder chooses the stallion. A beekeeper watches the queen leave and hopes. The fathers of the next generation are chosen in the sky, by chance, from every hive and hollow tree within several kilometers, and the beekeeper is not consulted. That is the first way out.

The second happens every spring. When a colony is strong, it divides. The old queen leaves with a large part of the workers, sometimes tens of thousands of bees, and they hang in a cluster on a branch while a few hundred scouts fan out to look for a new home. Thomas Seeley, a biologist at Cornell, spent decades studying what happens next, and described it in his book Honeybee Democracy. The scouts find ten or twenty candidate sites, come back and dance for them, and argue, through dancing, for hours or days. When about fifteen scouts are gathered at one site, the decision is made and the whole swarm lifts off for it. An empty box in the bee yard is one candidate among many. Often it loses. A swarm you fail to catch is simply gone, and almost every beekeeper alive has lost one.

So what did nine thousand years with the bee actually change? Mostly the box. In 1851 a minister in Pennsylvania named Lorenzo Langstroth noticed that bees leave a gap of a very particular size open: more than six millimeters and less than nine. Anything narrower, they glue shut. Anything wider, they fill with honeycomb. Build a hive with frames hung exactly that far apart and the bees leave the frames loose, so a person can lift them out one by one. He got a patent for it on October 5, 1852, and almost every modern hive is built on that measurement. The smoker is the same kind of trick. It hides the alarm signal. When a guard bee stings, she releases a chemical, the same one that gives a ripe banana its smell, and it tells every bee nearby to attack. Smoke swamps that signal, so the others never get the message.

We changed the box. We never changed the bee. People have tried. In 1916, at Buckfast Abbey in Devon, a disease blamed on a tiny mite that lives in the bees' breathing tubes killed thirty of the abbey's forty-six colonies. The only survivors were mixes of Italian bees and the native dark bee. A young monk named Karl Kehrle, known for the rest of his life as Brother Adam, spent the next seventy-six years trying to build a better bee from that start. But the queens still mated in the sky with whatever drones were up there, so in 1925 he set up a mating station on Dartmoor, far from other hives, where the only drones in the air were his own. Choosing the father of a bee meant moving the mating itself out of reach of every other hive.

In 1927, at Cornell, a researcher named Lloyd Watson took the other route. He tied a queen to an angled wooden board with loops of silk thread, put her under a microscope, and used a tiny syringe to place a drone's sperm inside her by hand. It was the first time a human being had chosen a honeybee's mate directly and made it work. It took until the 1940s to make the method dependable. Today it is used for a small number of breeding queens. The rest still mate in the sky.

Breeding left the dog and the cow with less variety than their wild ancestors. In 2012, Brock Harpur and colleagues at York University in Toronto compared the DNA of managed honeybees, the ones in beekeepers' boxes, with the wild populations they came from, and published the result in the journal Molecular Ecology. The managed bees carried more genetic variety than their ancestors. Beekeepers had spent centuries shipping different kinds of honeybee around the world, and because they could not control who mated with whom, everything mixed with everything.

The clearest proof of how little we control came from a laboratory in Brazil. In 1956 the geneticist Warwick Kerr brought several dozen queens of the African honeybee to São Paulo state, hoping to breed a bee better suited to the tropics than the European bees Brazilian beekeepers used. The African queens were kept behind grids that let workers out but held the queens in. In October 1957 a visiting beekeeper, for reasons still argued about, removed the grids. Twenty-six queens left with small swarms and went into the forest. Their descendants mated in the sky with everything they met, spread north through South and Central America at three to five hundred kilometers a year, and reached Texas in 1990. The press named them killer bees. Within a generation Brazilian beekeepers had learned to work with the new bee, and Brazil climbed from around forty-third among the world's honey producers to around seventh. Kerr's few dozen queens had become a wild population that spans two continents.

Bees had left a box and taken a continent once before. The honeybee is native to Africa, Europe and western Asia. There were none in the Americas until Europeans shipped them over; the first hives on record reached Jamestown, Virginia, in 1622. Swarms left those hives and spread west ahead of the settlers, sometimes by decades. Every wild honeybee in North America descends from bees somebody once shipped across an ocean in a box. The boxes still move. Four hundred years on, the largest pollination job on the planet happens every February in California. Nearly 1.7 million hives were trucked into California in 2025 to pollinate the almond orchards, according to the California State Beekeepers' Association. The state itself has only about 840,000 colonies. We can move the box anywhere. So each winter, hives are loaded onto trucks in Florida, the Dakotas and Texas, driven across the country, set down among the trees for about a month, and driven home.

If every wild bee on the continent came out of a box, what still separates the two? It is about a mite, and about who survived it. In September 1987 a mite called Varroa, which feeds on bees, was found on hives in Florida. Over the next ten years it wiped out most of the wild honeybee colonies in North America, the ones living in hollow trees with no one to treat them. Managed colonies survived because beekeepers dosed them with chemicals that kill the mite, and most still need that treatment every year. Years later, Thomas Seeley, the swarm researcher, went back to a patch of woodland in New York called the Arnot Forest, where he had counted wild colonies before the mite. Those colonies seem to have crashed after Varroa arrived. But by the 2000s their numbers were back to about one colony per square kilometer, the same density as before. Left alone, the wild bees had bred their way to resistance in roughly the ten years between the mite's arrival and Seeley's return. They did it through traits like biting the mites and opening the brood cells where the mites breed. The bees in our boxes, the ones we protect, mostly have not become resistant, because every time we treat them we save the ones that would have died, and nothing forces them to change. The wild population did for itself what domestication was supposed to do. The managed one is waiting for us.

After nine thousand years and a hundred million boxes, we are no further along than the figure on that cave wall in Spain: we get to reach in, and the bee decides whether to stay. Somewhere this afternoon, a queen about a week old is climbing out of a white wooden box, rising over the trees to a patch of sky her keeper has never seen. There, among thousands of strangers, whichever drones catch her will father every worker that lives in that box next year.
