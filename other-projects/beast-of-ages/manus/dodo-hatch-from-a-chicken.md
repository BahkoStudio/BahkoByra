The dodo is coming back. That part is roughly true. But the part nobody puts in the headline is this: the egg it hatches from will trace back to a chicken. An actual chicken, carrying inside its own body reproductive cells that came from a pigeon and were built to carry a dead bird's genes. To understand why the plan runs through a farmyard animal, you have to start with what the dodo actually was, and how badly we misjudged it the first time.

For three hundred years, the dodo has been the world's go-to picture of an animal too stupid and too fat to survive contact with humans. That picture is mostly wrong. A 2017 study using the largest collection of dodo bones ever assembled put its weight at around twelve kilograms and its height at about seventy centimeters, roughly five times the weight of the biggest pigeon alive today. It was built for walking through dense island undergrowth, more athletic than the waddling caricature suggests. The fat, comical dodo in old paintings is now thought to be either an overfed captive bird shipped to Europe, or simply the fashion of the time, since painters liked to show livestock as plump and prosperous. And the fearlessness sailors mistook for stupidity was really an adaptation: the dodo evolved on Mauritius with no land predators at all, so it had never needed to be afraid of anything walking toward it on two legs.

There is a second dodo myth worth clearing up, because it shows how easily a good story can outrun the evidence. In 1977 an ecologist named Stanley Temple noticed that a Mauritius tree called the tambalacoque had almost stopped producing new seedlings. He proposed that its seeds needed to be scraped and crushed inside a dodo's gizzard before they could germinate, and that the tree was dying out along with it. He fed seventeen tambalacoque fruits to wild turkeys; of the ten that survived digestion, three sprouted, with no control group to compare them against. The idea became a textbook favorite. But it was built on very little: earlier studies from 1941 and 1946 had already shown the seeds sprout without any digestion, and a 2004 review by the Botanical Society of America called Temple's experiment flawed. The tree's real problem is introduced monkeys, which strip the fruit while it is still unripe and destroy the seed inside, cutting germination by roughly forty times. It just happened to die out at the same time people were looking for someone, or something, to blame.

That was also exactly what killed it. Dutch sailors first recorded the dodo around 1598. The last confirmed sighting was in 1662, on a small islet off Mauritius; a few isolated birds may have held on into the 1690s. Adult dodos were hunted for meat, but the real damage came from what arrived on the ships with the sailors: pigs, dogs, cats, rats, and monkeys, none of which had ever existed on Mauritius before. The dodo nested on the ground, because it had never needed to nest anywhere else, and those animals ate the eggs and the chicks. In under a century, an animal that had never faced a predator in its life was extinct.

So which living bird do you even build one from? In 2002, a team led by Beth Shapiro pulled DNA out of a dodo specimen kept at Oxford and found a surprising answer. It was a small, elegant, fully flighted forest pigeon from Southeast Asia called the Nicobar pigeon. A later full genome, sequenced from a specimen in Copenhagen, confirmed it. The working theory is that some ancestor of the Nicobar pigeon flew to Mauritius, found an island with nothing on the ground that could hurt it, and over many generations simply stopped needing to fly. Over time it grew larger and lost the use of its wings, eventually becoming the dodo. Shapiro, now Colossal Biosciences' chief science officer, is the same scientist who found that connection more than two decades ago, and she now runs the team trying to reverse it.

Here is where it gets strange, and where the plan runs into biology instead of history. You cannot clone a bird the way you clone a mammal. Mammal cloning works by taking the nucleus out of an egg cell and replacing it with the nucleus you actually want; the technique that made Dolly the sheep. A bird's egg cell is enormous and packed with opaque yolk, and finding its nucleus inside all that yolk is close to physically impossible. It's a bit like trying to find a single white marble in a bowl of milk. So birds need a completely different route in.

That route is a kind of cell most people have never heard of: the primordial germ cell, or PGC. In a bird embryo just a few days old, before it looks anything like a bird, a small set of cells is already set aside to become that individual's future eggs or sperm. What makes PGCs strange and useful is how they get to their destination: they travel there through the bloodstream, migrating from one part of the embryo to the gonads, the organs that will later make its eggs or sperm. Because they travel through the blood, they can be pulled out of a young embryo, grown in a lab dish, and genetically edited. Then they can be injected into a completely different embryo, where they find their own way to the new gonads, exactly as if they had grown there all along.

In September 2025, Colossal announced it had done something nobody had managed before: grown pigeon PGCs in culture for an extended period, sixty days, with the cells doubling roughly every day and a half. Before that, only chicken and goose PGCs could reliably be kept alive and multiplying outside a body, which had quietly capped how much genetic engineering was possible in birds at all. The team said it tried more than three hundred combinations of growth factors before finding one that worked. It proved the method first on the common street pigeon, and is now applying the same technique to actual Nicobar pigeons, a breeding colony the company keeps in Texas.

Now bring in the chicken. This part of the plan borrows a trick poultry researchers have used for decades to help endangered birds. The method dates to 1969, when researchers first moved germ cells between turkey and chicken embryos. PGCs taken from one species can be injected into the embryo of another, and the second bird, called a surrogate host, will carry those foreign germ cells to its own gonads and let them mature there. In a widely cited conservation case decades later, PGCs from the endangered Houbara bustard were injected into ordinary white leghorn chicken embryos. Well over half of the injected embryos hatched successfully, and dozens of the resulting chickens grew up carrying functioning Houbara reproductive cells inside otherwise completely normal chicken bodies. Those chickens never stopped being chickens. What changed is what came out of them once they were grown: eggs or sperm carrying another species entirely. That same trick is exactly what Colossal is now doing with a Nicobar pigeon and a chicken.

Here is that plan in full. Using that dodo genome, Colossal intends to take Nicobar pigeon PGCs, edit them with the genetic changes that separate a Nicobar pigeon from a dodo, and inject the edited cells into chicken embryos. Those chickens grow up looking, behaving, and clucking exactly like chickens, because genetically, apart from the germ cells riding along inside them, they are chickens. When they reach maturity, though, some of their eggs or sperm will carry that edited, dodo-directed genetic material, which is the generation that is actually meant to produce something closer to a dodo.

There is a second, separate piece of engineering running alongside this one, and it is worth knowing so you do not confuse the two. In May 2026, Colossal announced it had hatched twenty-six healthy chicks from an entirely artificial egg: a 3D-printed shell that let oxygen and moisture pass the way a real one does. That project is a separate, later-stage engineering track, aimed further down the road at the problem of physically incubating an egg no living bird could sit on. It matters most for the giant moa, whose egg would need to be around eighty times the volume of a chicken's. For the dodo, an artificial egg may eventually matter too. For now, the harder and earlier problem is still the one running through a chicken's own reproductive system.

Assuming any of this works, there is already a plan for where the bird would go. Colossal announced the dodo project in January 2023 and partnered with the Mauritian Wildlife Foundation later that year to plan an actual return to Mauritius. The candidates are specific. They are places where the animals that ate the original dodo's eggs can be kept out, or never got established at all. Candidates include patches of restored forest in Black River Gorges National Park, and small offshore islands such as Round Island and Ile aux Aigrettes. Colossal's dodo project also lists a side benefit that has nothing to do with the dodo directly. The same genetic tools are meant to run a "genetic rescue" for Mauritius's own endangered pink pigeon, a species that is still alive today.

It is worth being honest about how far this still is from an actual dodo. As of the most recent public update, the closest anyone has come is a chicken carrying edited cells toward a distant hatch. By 2025, Colossal's breakthrough was in cell culture: pigeon PGCs, previously impossible to keep alive and dividing, can now be grown outside a body for months at a time. By 2026, the breakthrough was mechanical: chicks that survived inside a shell no bird had laid. Its woolly mammoth project has a public target of 2028; the dodo has no announced date. The underlying data behind the artificial-egg hatch numbers has also not yet been published, and no outside lab has independently verified it, which leaves years of biology still separating this from a living bird walking around on Mauritius.

But somewhere in a lab in Texas right now, there are chickens carrying, inside them, the beginnings of a species that has not existed since before the United States did. Their bodies do not know what they are carrying. They will live and act like any other chicken, right up until they lay the egg that changes everything.
