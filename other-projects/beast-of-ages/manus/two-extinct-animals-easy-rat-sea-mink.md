## Del 2: Manuset

*2 098 ord, cirka 14 minuter. Snittmening 16,3 ord, längsta 37, noll tankstreck. Skrivs och läses på engelska. CTA:n ingår som sista rad.*

In 2022, scientists recovered about 95 percent of an extinct rat's genome from two museum skins, and that still wouldn't be enough to bring the rat back.

On paper, that rat should be one of the easiest de-extinction cases on Earth. It died out only about 120 years ago, and a close living relative, the brown rat, is one of the most studied animals in science. There's a second animal like that, the sea mink of New England, whose living cousin is still everywhere. Yet not a single sea mink skin survives.

Neither animal is coming back, and the rat's problem turns out to be the mammoth's problem too.

Start with the rat. It lived on Christmas Island, a speck of limestone in the Indian Ocean about 350 kilometers south of Java. The naturalist Charles Andrews spent about ten months there in 1897 and 1898, and he wrote down what the nights sounded like. "This species is by far the commonest of the mammals found in the island," he wrote. "In every part I visited, it occurred in swarms." After sunset, "the whole forest is filled with its peculiar querulous squeaking and the noise of frequent fights." Querulous means whining, complaining.

The animal making that noise was Maclear's rat, also called the Christmas Island rat, first described in 1887. Its body was about 22 centimeters long, its tail was even longer, and its fur was long and coarse, with some hairs more than five centimeters long.

They had almost no fear of people. If you held out a lantern in the bush, Andrews wrote, "they will approach to examine the new phenomenon." They ran over sleeping men, raided the tents, and chewed up any boots left lying around. They could "climb as well as a squirrel," all the way to the tops of the papaya trees.

Within about five years of Andrews leaving, they were gone.

What killed them comes later. First, why would scientists pick this rat? A genome is an animal's full set of DNA. Old DNA comes out of museum specimens in tiny broken pieces, and a living relative's genome serves as the map for putting those pieces back in order. The brown rat's genome is one of the best-mapped of any mammal. The way to rebuild the extinct rat would be to edit a brown rat cell's DNA until it matches, then let a brown rat mother carry the pup. Rats have been cloned since 2003. Tom Gilbert, a geneticist at the University of Copenhagen, called it "the perfect case because when you sequence the genome, you have to compare it to a really good modern reference."

Gilbert's group put that to the test in 2022. They took two dry skins from the Oxford University Museum of Natural History, read their DNA more than 60 times over, and matched the pieces against the brown rat's genome.

In the end, about 95 percent of the extinct rat's genome could be recovered. That number is easy to misread. It doesn't mean the two rats are 95 percent similar. It means that about five percent of the extinct rat's genome could not be recovered at all, even with a nearly perfect map.

More than 1,600 genes came back with gaps, and 26 were missing entirely. The missing five percent falls hardest on two jobs: the immune system, and the sense of smell. The genes for its coat came through well. Of 86 genes that build hair, 83 came back almost whole. So you could probably rebuild most of its long, coarse fur. You couldn't fully rebuild its sense of smell, or its defenses against disease. Wherever the old DNA is missing, a rebuilt rat would carry the brown rat's version.

The missing smell genes, the paper said, are the kind a rat likely needs to survive in the wild. Smell is how a rat finds food, avoids predators and chooses a mate. As Gilbert told CBS News: "We've sequenced the hell out of it, we're still lacking five percent."

In January 2026, the same group went back to the data. The black rat is a closer relative than the brown rat. But even matched against it, about three and a half percent of the genome still couldn't be recovered. In computer simulations, reading the DNA 100 times over still left three to four percent missing. Their verdict: it is "unrealistic" to expect gene editing to give an animal a genome 100 percent identical to an extinct one's.

That's the first wall: some of the DNA can't be read at all. There's a second one. Maclear's rat and the brown rat split from a shared ancestor roughly two and a half million years ago. Their DNA may differ in many thousands of spots, or even far more. Gene-editing tools, the authors wrote in 2022, can typically change "several tens to several hundreds" of those spots at a time.

That's the mammoth's problem too. To make a mammoth, scientists start from an Asian elephant, and a mammoth and an Asian elephant are about as far apart genetically as Maclear's rat and the brown rat.

And the third wall: if you could build the rat, it would have to go back to the island where it died. So how did it die?

In 1899, the year phosphate mining began on the island, a ship called the SS Hindustan arrived with a cargo of hay. According to the mining company's manager, black rats came ashore with it. In late 1901, a British disease expert named Herbert Durham visited and found the black rats had "multiplied to huge numbers." The Maclear's rats he caught near the settlement were full of trypanosomes, tiny parasites that live in the blood.

A medical officer later told Andrews that around 1902 or 1903 he had seen some of the native rats "crawling about the paths in the daytime, apparently in a dying condition." Maclear's rats only ever came out at night. In 1904, Karl Hanitsch of the Raffles Museum in Singapore arrived with several dozen traps to thin out the native rats. He later wrote: "But there were none. The rats had disappeared." When Andrews came back in 1908, he declared Maclear's rat extinct, along with the island's other native rat, a burrowing cousin called the bulldog rat.

In 2008, a team including Gilbert tested museum rats from before and after the black rats arrived. Three native rats collected in 1888, all of them bulldog rats, showed no sign of the parasite. Afterward, six of 18 rats tested showed signs of it, black rats and Maclear's rats alike. The team called it the first time DNA had caught a disease spreading through a mammal that had never been exposed to it, right before that mammal died out. The authors stop short of calling it proof, since they had only a handful of specimens. But it fits what Durham saw in 1901.

And remember the black rat, the closer relative? It's also the animal that probably brought the disease.

Black rats still live on Christmas Island, along with feral cats and supercolonies of yellow crazy ants that can pack a thousand ants into a single square meter. In October 2025, the IUCN, the group that keeps the world's official list of threatened species, declared the island's native shrew extinct. One threat it listed was a blood-borne parasite carried by black rats.

The 2022 authors saw one possible upside. Keeping the brown rat's immune genes, they suggest, "could even have some potential benefit" on today's island. The catch is that every borrowed gene would make the rebuilt rat less like the one that lived there.

"We aren't actually planning to do it," Gilbert told the website Gizmodo, "as probably the world doesn't need any more rats."

Now, the sea mink. On paper, it looks even easier.

It lived along the rocky coast of New England, and possibly into Canada's Maritime provinces. Its closest living relative, the American mink, is common across North America, and in 2025 its full DNA was mapped in fine detail. And some of the people who knew the animal doubted it was a separate species at all.

One of them was Manly Hardy, a fur buyer from Maine who handled tens of thousands of mink skins. In 1903, he wrote that many of the mink on the coastal islands were "fully twice as large as the mink from inland," and that people called them "as large as small cats." Their fur was coarser and redder, and their skins had a smell of their own. "I could with my eyes shut pick them out from the woods mink," he wrote, "by their peculiar smell." But Hardy thought they were just one of many local varieties of mink. If he was right, turning an American mink into a sea mink might take only a few changes.

Their size made them valuable. A fur coat took only about half as many sea mink pelts as ordinary mink pelts. In the 1860s, when a mink skin sold for eight or ten dollars, hunters went from island to island with dogs, shovels, crowbars, and supplies of pepper and sulfur. If a mink hid in a crack in the rocks and its eyes could be seen shining, it was shot and dragged out with an iron rod with a screw on the end. If it couldn't be seen, they fired pepper into the hole or smoked it out with burning sulfur. Hardy wrote that the hunters had wiped them out, or very nearly. The last one was probably killed around 1880.

Not one sea mink skin survives. The skins were sold for fur, and no zoologist ever collected one. For decades, people thought one skin had survived: a stuffed mink, held up as the last sea mink, said to have been taken on Campobello Island, New Brunswick, in 1894. About 20 mammal experts examined it at the Smithsonian in 1965. In his 1966 report, Richard Manville concluded it was an unusually large American mink. In 2024, a team writing in the journal Archives of Natural History tested its DNA. The DNA they read was an exact match to the American mink.

What's left of the sea mink is bone: skull pieces, jaws and other bones. Most of them come from shell heaps left by Native peoples along the coast of Maine, and some are up to about 5,000 years old. Bonnie Newsom, a Penobscot archaeologist, told Nautilus magazine that the shells preserved them: "Without those, we probably wouldn't have any evidence of sea mink." Many of the skulls are broken open, because people ate the animals.

So was Hardy right? Scientists still can't agree on what the sea mink was. In 1903, Daniel Prentiss named it a new species, from a single skull fragment. Decades later, Hardy's daughter, Fannie Hardy Eckstorm, called its size "only an individual difference," meaning some mink simply grow bigger than others. In that same 1966 report, Manville made it a subspecies, a local variety of the American mink. And in April 2025, a team writing in the journal Quaternary Research studied more than 1,200 bones and concluded it was a separate species after all, a land predator that had started turning into a sea one. If they're right, it could take far more than a few changes.

None of those studies used DNA. A team at the University of Oklahoma has been working to read sea mink DNA from those bones, but so far, none of that DNA has been published.

Meanwhile, researchers from that Oklahoma team report that the American mink has slowly moved into the coastal and island parts of Maine where the sea mink used to live. A Maine wildlife biologist, Shevenell Webb, told the same magazine that trappers notice the mink on today's coast are "so much bigger out there." Until someone publishes sea mink DNA, nobody can say how close these bigger coastal mink come to the old ones.

So if someone tried anyway, you'd get a rat wrapped in the long, coarse coat of Maclear's rat, with a sense of smell and an immune system that are partly brown rat. And for the sea mink, you'd start from an American mink, the same kind of animal that was held up for decades as the last sea mink.

These were supposed to be the easy ones.
