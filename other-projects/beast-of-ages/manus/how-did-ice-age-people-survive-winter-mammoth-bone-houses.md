## Del 2: Manuset

In 1965, a villager in central Ukraine was digging a cellar in his yard when he hit a mammoth bone.

He had found a house. Under the village of Mezhyrich, archaeologists eventually uncovered four of them, round huts built from the skulls, jaws and tusks of mammoths. A dating study published in 2025 places one of them, Dwelling 4, at about 18,000 years old. It was built near the end of the coldest stretch of the last Ice Age, when the ground below the surface stayed frozen all year. Winters, according to a University of Exeter summary of research on the region, reached minus 20 Celsius or colder.

So how do you get through a winter like that, in a house made of bones?

The answer comes in layers: the walls, the ground, the fire, the clothes, and the food. And at the end, there's a ring of at least 64 mammoths that doesn't fit the story at all.

Start with the world outside the door.

Around 20,000 years ago, ice sheets covered much of northern Europe. South of the ice, the flat land that runs from Ukraine deep into Russia, known as the Russian Plain, was cold country with few trees. Below the soil lay permafrost, ground that stays frozen year round. Archaeologist Alexander Pryor of the University of Exeter has pointed out that most places this far north in Europe had been abandoned by the time the coldest stretch set in. The people who stayed had to find food, water and, above all, shelter. Birch and willow grew along the rivers, but out on the open grassland there was little wood to build with. What the land did have was mammoths.

So people built with them, and they built to a plan.

At a site called Gontsy, also in Ukraine, one hut used 46 mammoth skulls, 62 tusks, and 48 shoulder blades. Hold on to those 46 skulls. Where you get 46 mammoth skulls is its own story, and we'll get to it.

The builders made holes through shoulder blades. They hollowed out and sharpened long bones and tusks, so one piece could slot into the next. The French and Ukrainian team that began digging at Gontsy in 1993 called these huts the first clear case in human history of a standard building design. Archaeologists found the same plan at other sites.

The foundation was a ring of skulls, set into the ground with the tusk sockets buried for grip. On top went more skulls, shoulder blades, pelvises and long bones. Lower jaws were nested into each other in stacks. At Mezhyrich, archaeologists reconstructed one house with a base of 25 skulls and an outer wall of jaws stacked two to five high, like a row of tilted bowls. The houses there were about six meters across.

That's the frame. Next came the covering.

The bones were most likely the skeleton of the house, and the skin was literally skin. At Kostenki, a cluster of more than twenty Ice Age sites on the Don River in Russia, excavators found flat mammoth bones scattered across the floor of one dwelling. They took them to be weights that held down a roof of hides.

Then came the cheapest material of all: dirt.

Around the huts at Gontsy, people dug pits. One job of those pits was to provide loess, the fine windblown soil of the plain. The team says it was dug out "to plug and insulate the walls of the huts against the cold." Packed against the hides and bones, it sealed the gaps and blocked the wind.

The floor helped too. That same dwelling at Kostenki had its floor dug 30 to 56 centimeters below the old ground surface. A sunken floor keeps you lower than the wind.

Now the fire.

At Gontsy, the hearths sit right inside the huts, and the team found "numerous hearths with bone ash." But fuel was the hard part on a plain with so little wood. The answer was the same animal. People burned bone.

The Gontsy team lists fresh bones as fuel, and fresh bone still holds grease, which likely helped it burn. That dwelling at Kostenki had no hearth inside, but its floor held six kilograms of burnt bone. A pit at Mezhyrich contains what researchers classed as "bone fuels." Wood was still used where people could get it, and charcoal turns up too. Bone was the fuel the mammoths left behind.

Then there's what people wore.

To sew fitted clothing, you need a point to pierce hide, and these sites have them: awls, pointed tools for punching holes, and needles with an eye for thread. Gontsy has needles carved from ivory. Yudinovo, in Russia, has needles and needle cases made from the long bones of arctic foxes. The foxes tell their own story. At Yudinovo, fox paw bones turn up still joined, a sign the paws were probably left on the skins. The team there points out that fox fur is at its best at the start of winter, so that's most likely when the foxes were hunted. Gontsy's hunters also went after wolf, lynx and wolverine in winter for their fur. The excavators call fur processing a key activity at the site.

And the last layer is food.

Those same pits at Gontsy had a second job. The team says they were also used "to store food in summer on the permafrost at the bottom of the pit." The frozen ground was the freezer.

And the camp ran on the seasons. The camp was lived in from the end of one winter to the end of the next. Reindeer were hunted in spring, marmots in summer and early autumn, and fur animals in winter. A pit at Mezhyrich holds food and butchery waste, mainly from mammoth, hare and fox. And another bone-house site in Ukraine, Dobranichivka, is described in the Encyclopedia of Ukraine as a winter settlement.

So picture one winter night. You step down onto a floor dug below the old ground. Hides stretch over a frame of skulls and jaws. Loess is packed against the wall, sealing the gaps. A fire of fresh bone burns in the hearth. Your clothes are fur, stitched with an ivory needle. And in a pit outside, meat sits on the permafrost, frozen solid. Put together, it's a system, and it worked well enough that people across the region kept building to the same plan.

Now, back to those 46 skulls. Some of you are thinking: did they hunt all those animals?

Fair question. The answer is different at each site.

At Gontsy, the team believes a whole herd of mammoths died naturally one winter in nearby gullies, mostly females and young animals. When the snow melted, people found the carcasses and set up camp next to them. That gave them building material, fresh bones for fuel, ivory for tools, and probably frozen meat. People took nearly 80 percent of the bones.

Mezhyrich tells a mixed story. The 2025 dating study, drawing on earlier work, reports that Dwelling 4 used bones from at least 37 mammoths. Most were collected from natural bone piles, though at least one animal seems to have been freshly dead.

Yudinovo points to hunting. A 2008 study there argued that the bones came from freshly killed animals, many of them adult females in their prime, averaging about two tons each.

And in July 2026, a team led by researchers at Stockholm University tested DNA from 521 mammoths. At sites where mammoths died naturally, about two thirds were male. At eleven bone piles left by humans, dating from about 30,000 to 20,000 years ago, it flipped: about seven in ten were female. The study's lead author explained that "female-led herds moved across the landscape in more predictable ways than solitary males." That would have made them easier for hunters to find. The researchers concluded that "human activity was the primary driver" behind those piles.

And the people who built these houses were remarkably few. The Gontsy team estimates that they lived in groups of about 30, spread across a territory of roughly 500,000 square kilometers, about the size of Spain. Their computer models put the total at just 100 to 500 groups. Yet they were connected. Across their territory, shells, some from the Black Sea, were carried as far as 600 kilometers, most likely passed from group to group.

Which brings us back to that ring of 64 mammoths from the start. It's about 7,000 years older than the houses at Mezhyrich and Gontsy.

At Kostenki, starting in 2014, Pryor's team excavated a different structure: a ring about 12 meters across, twice the width of a house at Mezhyrich. It holds close to 3,000 bones from at least 64 mammoths. It's the oldest mammoth-bone structure built by modern humans yet found on the Russian Plain. Radiocarbon dating, which measures how long ago something died, shows that two of the jawbones were roughly 200 to 1,200 years older than the other dated material. Some bones were already old when they were stacked.

It looks like the ultimate mammoth house. The team that dug it has doubts.

"It is difficult to imagine how an area this large could have been roofed," Pryor said. The ring had few tools inside, fewer than you'd expect where people lived for long. And it would have been, in his words, "smelly," drawing in wolves and foxes. His team found burnt bone, wood charcoal, and soft plant tissue of the kind found in edible roots, plus three pits at the edge. One idea is that it was a place to store and process the meat from many mammoths. Another idea is ritual, though Pryor admits archaeology alone can't tell us what kind. And the reason anyone was there at all may have been water. There might have been a natural spring nearby that stayed unfrozen all winter. Remember Pryor's list: food, water and shelter. At Kostenki, the bones may have been about the water.

Clive Gamble, one of the archaeologists on that project, put it bluntly in 2023: "whatever else it is, it is not a house."

The huts at Mezhyrich are half that width, small enough to roof with hides, and the 2025 dating study still reads them as homes, though that is debated too. The same study notes that the way of life there is believed to have ended abruptly about 13,000 years ago, around the time the local mammoths disappeared.

Dwelling 4 was left where it was found, under a shelter built over it. In 2010, heavy snow nearly brought down that shelter. In 2015, archaeologist Pavlo Shydlovskyi warned that within a few years it could lose its value as a museum object. In March 2025, archaeologists from Kyiv University scanned it in 3D.

The house that kept people alive through Ice Age winters now needs protecting from the snow.
