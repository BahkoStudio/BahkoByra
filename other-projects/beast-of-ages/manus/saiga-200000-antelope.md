May 2015. Central Kazakhstan. On an open plain of grass and dust, a saiga antelope stops in the middle of a step. Her head drops. Her legs shake, fold, and she goes down. She does not get up. A few meters away, another animal stumbles. Then another. By evening, the ground around the herd is covered with still bodies.

There are no wounds on them. No blood, no bite marks, no torn skin. No wolf tracks lead in or out. From a distance, the animals look asleep. Within days, whole valleys look like this. Quiet, still, dotted with pale shapes all the way to the horizon.

In three weeks, more than 200,000 antelope are dead. That is more than half of every saiga antelope on Earth. Whole herds, wiped from the map between one week and the next. And nobody can explain it.

To understand why this made no sense, you first have to meet the animal it happened to. Because if any creature on Earth had earned the right to be called unkillable, it was this one.

The saiga is a small antelope with thin legs, a sandy coat, and the strangest face in the animal kingdom. We will get to the face. First you need to know what this animal has already walked through.

The saiga is a leftover from the ice age. Herds of saigas grazed in the shadow of the woolly mammoths, heads down in the frozen grass. Its neighbors were woolly rhinos and cave lions. The plain they shared was cold, dry, and dusty, a hard world that built hard animals. The saiga answered it by staying small, staying fast, and never standing still. A saiga herd is always moving, drifting across the grass in long lines, eating as it goes.

Then the world warmed, and the giants began to fall. The woolly rhino went, and the cave lion with it. The mammoth held on longest of all, and then it went too. One by one, the great beasts of the ice age dropped away, like lights going out across a plain at night. The little antelope with the strange face kept running.

Then came people. Hunters followed the herds for thousands of years, on foot and on horseback, with arrows and later with rifles. The saiga outran them and held on. Winters on the steppe kill weak animals every year. Droughts starve the grass. Wolves take the calves. The saiga absorbed all of it and kept going. Empires rose and fell around it. Borders moved, maps were redrawn, and the herds kept walking through it all. It outlasted the Soviet Union itself.

Then, in the 1990s, came the hardest blow it had ever taken. The Soviet Union collapsed, borders opened, and poachers swept the steppe, killing saigas for their horns. Only the males carry horns, and the horns were what sold. Within about a decade, around 90 percent of all the saigas alive had been wiped out. Ninety out of every hundred animals, gone. It looked like the end of the species.

The saiga refused. The survivors kept breeding, and the herds crawled back from the edge, one calving season at a time.

That is the record of this animal. Scientists have a word for a creature like this. A relict. A living piece of a world that no longer exists. It outlived the mammoth and outlasted the ice. In living memory it stood at the edge of extinction and stepped back. Ask what kills a saiga, and history gives a strange answer. Almost nothing. The ice could not finish it. The rifles could not finish it. The saiga survives. Above everything else, that is what this animal does. It survives.

Now look at the face. The saiga's nose is a swollen, drooping trunk that hangs down over its mouth. It looks wrong. It looks like the animal borrowed it from something much bigger and never gave it back. It is one of the finest noses nature ever built. In summer, the moving herds kick up clouds of dust, and that nose filters the dust before it reaches the lungs. In winter, the same nose warms the freezing air with every breath. A filter in the hot months, a heater in the cold ones. That nose helped carry this small antelope through the ice age. Remember the nose. This story ends inside it.

So that is the animal that lay dead across the steppe in May 2015. The great survivor, down by the tens of thousands, with no enemy in sight.

And there was a detail that made scientists uneasy. They had seen this before. In 1984, saigas died in huge numbers. It happened again in 2010, and again in 2012. Some suspected a germ, but nothing was ever proven. Each time, the dying stopped as suddenly as it began. Each time, the file closed without an answer.

In 2015, one thing was different. Scientists were standing in the middle of the herds when it started. A monitoring team, including the ecologist Steffen Zuther, had come to the Betpak-Dala region for the calving season. Every spring, saiga mothers gather in huge groups and give birth within days of each other. The team was there to count newborn calves. Instead, they stood in the grass and watched the herd die around them. Animals that looked healthy in the morning were down by afternoon and dead by night. There was no treatment to give and no time to give it. Once an animal showed symptoms, it had hours left. The team took samples, sent word out, and kept counting. Within days, the calving ground they had come to study was silent.

The theories arrived before the lab results did. Baikonur, the great rocket launch site, sits on the same Kazakh steppe. Rocket fuel is toxic, and many people suspected a leak had poisoned the plains. Others expected a new virus, or poison in the plants or the water. Investigators tested the rocket fuel idea and ruled it out. The virus hunt came up empty. One by one, the easy answers died in the lab.

The real answer was stranger than all of them. It had been staring everyone in the face. Or rather, it had been hiding just behind it.

In 2018, a team led by Professor Richard Kock published the solution in the journal Science Advances. The killer had a name. Pasteurella multocida. A bacterium. It is one of the most ordinary germs in veterinary medicine, found in cattle, in birds, and in rabbits.

And the place they found it explains everything. The bacterium lives in the saiga's nose and throat. Healthy animals carry it. All of them do. Calves pick it up early and carry it for life. For generation after generation, it had done no harm at all. A passenger in the airways, there with every breath.

Then, in May 2015, something flipped a switch. In animal after animal, across herd after herd, the passenger left the airways and poured into the blood. The condition is called hemorrhagic septicemia. In plain words, the blood itself turns poisonous, and the body shuts down. That is why death came within hours. That is why there were no wounds.

No predator ever touched those herds. Every antelope that fell that May was killed by something it had carried its entire life. The killer was not an invader. It was a passenger. It was inside them all along.

Which leaves the real question. If the bacterium had always been there, why did it turn? Kock's team dug through years of weather records and field data, looking for anything unusual. One pattern stood out. In the days before the saigas started dying, the steppe had turned unusually warm and unusually humid. Heat and moisture together, hanging over the calving grounds. That was the trigger. Somehow, that shift in the air sent a harmless throat bacterium surging into the bloodstream of its host. The weather pulled the trigger, and the gun was already inside the animal.

The timing turned a disaster into a catastrophe. The outbreak struck during calving, the one time of year when saigas crowd together. Mothers pack side by side on open ground to give birth. Newborn calves lie in the grass beside them. Every adult on that ground carried the bacterium, and every adult stood under the same warm, wet air. So the switch flipped in every animal at the same time. Whole herds went down together. In the herds that were hit, almost no animal survived. Mothers died on the calving grounds, and their calves died with them. An entire generation, gone in the same week it was born.

No fence could have stopped it. No ranger could have shot it. The threat was already past every defense the herd had, because it lived inside the herd itself. When the counting was finished, about three weeks after the first animal fell, more than 200,000 saigas were dead. That was 88 percent of the Betpak-Dala population, nine of every ten animals in those herds. More than half of the entire species, erased in less than a month.

And the old mysteries of 1984, 2010, and 2012 suddenly looked different. A killer that rides inside every animal is always at the scene. It only has to wait for its weather.

Set the two halves of this story side by side. On one side, an antelope that outlived the mammoth, outlasted the ice, and walked away from the poaching wars of the nineties. On the other, a germ from its own nose that nearly finished the species in three weeks.

Surviving everything does not make an animal untouchable. Some killers never chase you. Some walk beside you your whole life, quiet as your own breath, and wait for the right weather.

But the saiga had one more turn left. The survivors of 2015 kept breeding, and the herds kept growing. By 2024, counts on the steppe passed 2.8 million animals. That is fourteen living saigas for every one that fell in 2015. In December 2023, the International Union for Conservation of Nature moved the saiga from critically endangered to near threatened. Eight years after that terrible May, the saiga climbed off the critical list. The survivor had done it again.

And the saiga is still out there. It walks the same steppe today, nose down in the grass, as if none of this ever happened. That animal carries the memory of a lost world in its body. Its strange nose was built for the mammoth steppe, a cold and dusty plain that once stretched from Europe to Alaska. The saiga shared that plain with woolly mammoths, woolly rhinos, and cave lions. Every one of those giants is gone. The small antelope with the impossible nose is still here. That leaves one question this video cannot answer. Why were the animals of the ice age so much bigger than the mammals alive today? What built giants then, and why did the giants lose? We made a whole video about that lost world. It is called Why Was Ice Age Megafauna So Much Bigger Than Today's Mammals? It is on your screen right now. Watch it, and you will meet the giants the saiga once ran beside. Their world is gone. The saiga remembers it.
