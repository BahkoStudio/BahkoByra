## Del 2: Manuset

*1 949 ord, cirka 13 minuter. Snittmening 13,9 ord, längsta 38, noll tankstreck. Skrivs och läses på engelska. CTA:n ingår som sista rad.*

On August 24th, TIME reported that the first woolly mammoth calves won't be born in 2028. The company behind them, Colossal Biosciences, is valued at more than 10 billion dollars. Ben Lamm, Colossal's chief executive, put the new date like this: "We are thinking it will be in the early 2030s. We don't have a hard date. Not 2036, but not 2030 either."

The woolly mammoth is still coming back. The math behind it just got a lot harder. When the project started, the plan called for editing about 60 genes in an elephant. According to TIME, that number is now about 150, "and rising." And under the gene problem sits a second one. An elephant pregnancy lasts almost two years, so a calf born in 2028 had to be on its way almost two years earlier.

How long had 2028 even been the date?

In September 2021, Colossal launched with the Harvard geneticist George Church as co-founder and 15 million dollars in first investment. The Guardian reported that the team hoped "to have their first set of calves in six years." TechCrunch reported the same day that the aim was "the next four to six years." Counted from the launch, that's 2025 to 2027. So 2028 was already later than the first window.

The earliest public mention of 2028 we could find is from March 2023. At the South by Southwest festival in Austin, Lamm was asked about a mammoth by 2028 and answered, "I think we're on the path for that." In February 2024, he and Colossal scientist Eriona Hysolli wrote in Newsweek: "Our first mammoth calves will be born in 2028."

In April 2025, the month Colossal unveiled its gene-edited "dire wolves," it gave TIME a precise milestone on the way to a 2028 birth. In the company's own words: "on track for our embryos to be ready for implantation by the end of 2026." That means ready to go inside an elephant mother. As late as March 2026, NPR reported that the company predicted the first mammoth "in about two years."

Less than six months later, the date was gone.

Colossal's own website hasn't caught up. When we checked on September 25th, two of its pages, last edited in April and May, still described 2028 as the target.

Why did the date fall apart so fast?

An Asian elephant carries a calf for up to about 22 months, the longest pregnancy of any mammal. So by our math, a calf born at the end of 2028 needed an embryo inside an elephant by early-to-mid 2027. That's exactly how the plan was built. Embryos ready by the end of 2026. A pregnancy in 2027. A birth in 2028.

In other words, 2028 only worked if an edited embryo went into an elephant within the next few months. On that same check, Colossal's news page and press releases announced no edited elephant embryo and no elephant pregnancy. That only covers what the company has published, and Colossal has kept quiet before. Its first two dire wolves were born in October 2024, and the world only heard about them in April 2025.

There is one public promise we can check, though. Colossal's plan needs elephant eggs: each embryo starts as an edited elephant cell placed inside one. In February 2026, Lamm said Colossal was more than two and a half years into work on collecting those eggs. And he said: "I think we're going to show some really interesting progress on that this year."

So what happened to the genes?

Every count we found was bigger than the one before. It was about 60 genes at the launch in 2021, and about 65 by 2023. In April 2025, Lamm told TIME the list had grown to 85, and the company said it had edited 25 of them. In August 2026, TIME reported roughly 150. The story gave no new count of how many are already edited.

The new genes come from closer study of what separates a mammoth from an elephant. TIME lists some of what Colossal found. Genes, and the switches that turn genes on and off, for an ear about one tenth the size of an elephant's. A shorter tail. Simple structures in elephant skin that resemble the oil glands a mammoth's coat would have needed. The team has also put pieces of mammoth DNA into living elephant skin cells. Those pieces work as switches. Lamm's own description: "It's a little weird and Frankensteiny, but we've done that."

He framed the delay as a gain in quality. "I hate to say this, because I don't want to call the mammoth a product," he told TIME, "but it will be a better product because of our deeper analysis of what truly made a mammoth a mammoth."

Colossal's explanation has changed, too. In September 2025, Lamm told Screen Rant: "Our goal is end of 2028. We're on track with that from the editing perspective." The biggest hold-up, he said, would be finding elephant mothers to carry the calves: "the surrogacy side and, less so, the science of it." In February 2026, he said the hold-up "won't be a technology factor." In TIME's August story, the reason for the delay is the science itself: more gene work than Colossal's teams expected.

Both explanations can be true at once. The genes got harder. And the surrogacy problem hasn't gone anywhere.

Colossal plans to have Asian or African elephants carry its first calves. Since 1986, the International Union for Conservation of Nature, the IUCN, has listed the Asian elephant as Endangered. A 2019 estimate put the wild population at around 48,000 to 52,000 animals. Asking an endangered elephant to carry an experimental, gene-edited calf is a very large request. George Church warned about this in 2017, before Colossal existed. "It would be unreasonable," he said, "to put female reproduction at risk in an endangered species."

His answer back then was an artificial womb, and Colossal has been working on artificial wombs. In January 2025, it said it had grown fertilized embryos of a marsupial more than halfway through pregnancy in an artificial womb. A marsupial is a pouched animal, like a kangaroo. In May 2026, it hatched chicks from a fully artificial egg, built for its project on the moa, a giant extinct bird from New Zealand. Those were a marsupial and a bird. An elephant calf would have to grow to around 200 pounds outside a body, for nearly two years.

How far along is the elephant work itself? What has been announced is still early. In March 2024, Colossal reported elephant stem cells: ordinary elephant cells turned into cells that can, in principle, become any cell in the body. It was hard. Elephants carry up to 19 extra copies of a cancer-fighting gene called TP53, and the team had to switch that defense down to make the cells cooperate. Church joked that elephants "might get the 'hardest to reprogram' prize." That work was shared as a preprint, a paper posted before independent review. We couldn't find it in a peer-reviewed journal by late September 2026.

The woolly mouse study from March 2025 was also a preprint. Its scientists edited seven genes at once and reported 38 pups, with edits aimed at woolly, mammoth-like hair. But Lamm himself said at the time: "The woolly mouse project doesn't bring us any closer to a mammoth, but it does validate the work we are doing."

The dire wolf project ran on a much shorter clock. Colossal edited grey wolf cells, and ordinary dogs carried the pups. A dog pregnancy lasts about two months. An elephant carries one calf for up to about 22 months, around ten times as long. That one difference explains more about the mammoth's timeline than any press release.

So where does the money stand? In January 2025, investors put 200 million dollars into Colossal, at a price that valued the whole company at 10.2 billion dollars. In July 2026, Axios reported talks that would value it at 20 to 30 billion dollars. When we checked, Colossal had announced no closed round. A month after that report, TIME published the new date: the early 2030s.

When a mammoth does arrive in the 2030s, what will it actually be?

Colossal answered that at its launch. Church said: "Our goal is to make a cold-resistant elephant, but it is going to look and behave like a mammoth." The recipe starts with an elephant. Colossal rewrites some of the elephant's own genes to match the mammoth versions. Beth Shapiro, Colossal's chief science officer, told NPR in March 2026: "It will be a mammoth because it will look like a mammoth and it will act like a mammoth."

Colossal has faced this question before, with the dire wolves. In May 2025, Shapiro told New Scientist: "Our animals are grey wolves with 20 edits that are cloned."

Two outside scientists say the mammoth would be an edited elephant, too. Vincent Lynch, an evolutionary biologist at the University at Buffalo, told NBC News: "It's not really a mammoth. It's a mutated Asian elephant." Tori Herridge, a mammoth expert now at the University of Sheffield, put it more gently in 2025: "A mammoth is much more than just an elephant in a fur coat." She also called the risk to an elephant mother "unjustifiable" for an experiment that would, at best, produce an imitation of a woolly mammoth.

In 2016, the IUCN's Species Survival Commission published guidelines on creating stand-ins for extinct species. They call the term "de-extinction" misleading, and they say "none of the current pathways will result in a faithful replica of any extinct species." One example they give is this exact one: cold-tolerant elephants as stand-ins for woolly mammoths. The IUCN also works with Colossal now. In September 2026, that same commission started a 4-million-dollar partnership with Colossal's charity arm, for species that are still alive.

Why build a mammoth at all? At its launch, Colossal pointed to the climate. The idea is that big grazers could trample snow and knock down trees, turning parts of the Arctic back into grassland. Snow keeps winter cold out of the ground. Packed-down snow lets that cold in, and that helps keep the permafrost from thawing. Permafrost is ground that stays frozen all year.

A 2020 study in Scientific Reports ran the numbers on the idea, with a team that included the Russian scientists Nikita and Sergey Zimov. It found that in theory, 80 percent of today's permafrost soils could survive to the year 2100 if enough large animals roamed them. That's a computer model. The Zimovs' own test site in Siberia, Pleistocene Park, already runs that experiment with horses, bison, musk oxen and reindeer.

How many mammoths would the climate plan take? Herridge put it this way in 2021: "You are talking about hundreds of thousands of mammoths which each take 22 months to gestate and 30 years to grow to maturity."

None of this means the mammoth isn't coming. Colossal has elephant stem cells, woolly mice, and more than 615 million dollars raised. It just won't be born when promised.

Work the pregnancy math backwards one more time. A calf in Lamm's window, after 2030 but before 2036, means an edited embryo inside an elephant mother sometime between about 2029 and 2033. Before that, Colossal needs elephant eggs. In February 2026, Lamm said he expected to show progress on collecting them "this year." As of late September 2026, Colossal had announced no egg result.

Colossal's giant bird has a mother problem too: no bird alive is big enough to hatch its egg. Watch 15 Facts About the De-Extinct Moa That Make No Sense next.
