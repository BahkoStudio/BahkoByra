## Del 2 — Manuset

*2 964 ord, cirka 20 minuter. Snittmening 13,2 ord, längsta 33, noll tankstreck.
Skrivs och läses på engelska. CTA:n ovan ingår som sista rad.*

Your breath hangs in the air. The grass under your feet is stiff with frost, and it crunches when you walk. But in front of you, a pond is steaming.

Kneel down and put your hand in. The water is almost as warm as a bath. And all around its rim, wherever that heat reaches, the grass is still green.

Something big is coming down the slope behind you. It's a mammoth. A young male, about twelve feet tall at the shoulder. Years ago he left the family he was born into, and today nobody older is walking with him. He's hungry, and the greenest grass for miles is growing right here, beside this water.

He puts one front foot on the red rock at the rim. The rock is wet. A few steps out, the water is deeper than he is tall.

Hold that picture. We'll come back to him.

This place is real. It sits at the southern end of the Black Hills of South Dakota, in a town named after its warm springs: Hot Springs. So far, at least 61 mammoths have been found in this one pond. Nearly all of them were males, and most of them were young.

So why did young males, one after another, walk into a pond they couldn't climb out of? What was life actually like for the animals around it? And there's a third question, one that took more than fifty years to get right: when did all of this happen?

Let's start with what we know for certain.

In June 1974, a heavy equipment operator named George Hanson was leveling a small hill for a housing development in Hot Springs. The landowner was a man named Phil Anderson. Hanson's blade hit something that shone white in the sunlight. It was a tusk, about seven feet long, sliced in half along its length.

George took some of the bones to his son, Dan, who had taken classes in geology and archaeology. One look told Dan the bones were important. Phil Anderson contacted three colleges in South Dakota and one university in Nebraska, and all four said no. Dan had better luck. He called his old college professor, Larry Agenbroad, a geologist at Chadron State College in Nebraska, and Agenbroad wanted in. Until help arrived, Dan kept watch over the site around the clock.

Agenbroad was busy on another mammoth dig in Arizona at the time, so he sent his colleague Jim Mead with part of the crew, and they spent ten days rescuing the bones. The next summer, in 1975, Agenbroad and Mead led a team of student volunteers in the first real excavation. They started, as Agenbroad later remembered, with "a roaring budget of $500."

By the end of that summer, Phil Anderson had made a decision. His 14 acres were worth more to science than to a housing development. With help from the community, the land became a nonprofit research site.

Early on, the bones also got a date. By 1980, radiocarbon tests had put them at about 26,000 years old, near the coldest part of the last ice age. If that's right, the frost you felt at the start belongs to the last ice age. Keep that number in mind.

So how does a warm pond end up inside a hill in South Dakota?

It starts deep underground. Far below the surface, there were layers of gypsum, a soft mineral that dissolves in water. Over a very long time, groundwater ate it away. The rock above started to collapse, piece by piece, until a column of broken rock reached all the way up to the surface. Then the ground caved in, and a sinkhole opened in the red rock at the top.

Next came the water. That column of broken rock made a perfect pipe for warm spring water rising from far below, and the water filled the hole. The pond it made was about 160 feet long and 80 feet wide, roughly the size of an Olympic swimming pool. The water was around 13 to 16 feet deep, deeper than an average mammoth was tall at the shoulder. The Mammoth Site's own tour puts its temperature at 90 to 95 degrees Fahrenheit.

Everything here starts with that warmth. The springs in town still run at roughly 80 to 90 degrees today. A 2013 study of the site in the French journal Paleo pictured the spring as "a green island in the heart of the steppe," the cold, dry grassland all around it. Close to half the pollen from the pond is grass, with trees like spruce, birch and oak somewhere nearby. There isn't much pollen in the mud, so this is a best guess. But the 2013 study pictured it as a place where, out on the cold, open plain, the grass kept growing all year.

That mattered to a mammoth. Almost all of them were Columbian mammoths. They were taller than the woolly mammoths most people picture, and they lived farther south. Above all, they ate grass. Larry Agenbroad and Jim Mead knew that firsthand. In a cave in Utah called Bechan Cave, they helped study a thick layer of ancient mammoth dung, and it turned out to be about 95 percent grass and grass-like plants called sedges.

Back at the pond, look at its walls. They sloped from about 30 degrees to nearly straight up, and they were lined with red shale, a rock made of hardened mud that turns slippery when it's wet. Agenbroad described the wet shale this way: "slick as snot on a glass doorknob."

The best grass for miles was growing on the rim of a trap.

Now, some of you are thinking: a grown Columbian mammoth weighed around ten tons. Elephants are strong swimmers. How does an animal that powerful get stuck in a pond the size of a swimming pool?

Fair question. Getting in was easy. The hard part was getting out. To climb out of deep water, a heavy animal has to get its front feet onto the bank and haul the rest of its body up after them. Here, the bank was wet shale, tilted steeply toward the water. Every time those front feet found the bank, they had almost nothing to grip.

The scientists who run the site today say the mammoths were alive when they went in. And a 2005 US Geological Survey report that Agenbroad co-wrote concluded that, unable to climb out, they starved or wore themselves out.

So what was life actually like around this water? Let's rebuild one day. The order is our guess, but every animal in it came out of the mud.

It's early morning at the rim. Steam is rolling off the water into the cold air. Out on the plain the ground is frozen hard, but here, inside the steam, the grass along the shore is green. In the warm shallows there are frogs and salamanders, fish, snails and clams. Birds walk the muddy margins and leave their footprints behind, and some of those tracks have been found.

Later in the morning, bigger animals come down to drink. One is Camelops, an extinct North American camel. Another is an extinct llama. There are pronghorn too, fast, antelope-like animals still found on the plains today. All of them turn up in the dig.

Smaller animals end up here too: rodents, lizards, snakes, even a badger. Many probably die on the shore and wash in later.

Around midday, a young male mammoth comes down to the green grass along the shore. We know mammoths walked here. Their footprints are preserved in the old pond's layers, pressed into mud that later hardened. He eats his way along the shore, then turns and climbs back up the slope. Most days, that's how it goes.

By late afternoon, something has been dead in the water for a while, and the smell carries. That smell, the 2005 report says, is probably what pulls in the meat eaters. Gray wolves and coyotes come. So does the biggest of them all, the short-faced bear, one of the largest meat-eating land mammals North America has ever had. We know it came, because its skull turned up here in 1983. As for how it ended up in the pond, the site's own tour offers two guesses. Either it "met its match with a not-so-dead mammoth," or it simply got trapped like everything else.

Night falls, and the pond keeps steaming in the dark. On the bottom, a little more mud settles over whatever went in that day.

More than 5,000 finds have been recorded here since 1974, from the mammoths and at least 87 other species. Now repeat that day, year after year, for as long as the pond stayed open. And it stayed open far longer than anyone guessed for fifty years.

So, why young males?

First, how do we even know they were male? The answer is in the pelvis, the big ring of bone at the hips. In a female elephant, the hole in the middle is wide, because a newborn calf has to fit through it. In a male, it's narrower. One skeleton at the site had no head, so it was nicknamed Marie Antoinette, after the French queen who lost hers. Its pelvis showed it was a male, so now he's called Murray. Agenbroad measured the Hot Springs pelvises and said all but one were male. In 1994, he and the British mammoth expert Adrian Lister checked them again, using a method Lister had worked out, and got the same result: nearly all male. The scientists who run the site today still describe them this way: they "appear to be males, and most represent younger individuals."

Age comes from the teeth. A mammoth went through six sets of big chewing teeth in its life, and scientists compare how worn they are with the teeth of African elephants whose ages are known. Depending on who's counting, most of these mammoths died somewhere between their teens and their late thirties. That's young for an animal that could live past sixty.

Compare Hot Springs with another famous mammoth site, in Waco, Texas. There, a group of females and their young died together, a family caught all at once. Hot Springs caught something different: young males, one after another.

Larry Agenbroad had a nickname for them: "teenybopper males." He also had a simple theory, and he put it the way only a father could. "Having raised teenage boys," he said, "I know teenagers are allergic to snow shovels." In winter, out on the open plain, getting to grass meant digging through snow. At the pond, the grass was green and free for the taking.

But there's a deeper answer, and it comes from elephants alive today.

In an elephant family, the leader is usually the oldest female, the matriarch. Around her are her relatives and their calves. In Amboseli National Park in Kenya, where Cynthia Moss and her team have followed the same families since 1972, young males leave home somewhere between the ages of 10 and 16.

Leaving means losing the matriarch's experience. In 2011, Karen McComb of the University of Sussex and her colleagues played recorded lion roars to elephant families. Male lions are the real danger to elephant calves. Families led by younger matriarchs tended to under-react to the male roars. The older the matriarch, the stronger the family's response. Our young male at the rim left his matriarch behind years ago.

Most woolly mammoth finds are male, too. In 2017, a team at the Swedish Museum of Natural History read the sex from the DNA of 98 woolly mammoth remains. Sixty-nine percent were male. Their explanation sounds a lot like Hot Springs: young males traveling without an experienced matriarch were more likely to die in natural traps.

There's one more piece to this. Back in the 1990s, rangers in Pilanesberg National Park in South Africa had a problem. The park's young male elephants were attacking rhinos, and more than 40 rhinos were killed. Those young males were orphans, and the park had no grown males, which are called bulls. In 2000, Rob Slotow and his colleagues described what happened when six older bulls were brought in. The young males calmed down, and the killing stopped.

Young males, it turns out, behave differently when there's nobody older around.

So what about the ones that don't fit? There are a few. The most complete skeleton at the site belongs to a mammoth the staff call Napoleon. By its teeth, it was about 47 years old when it died. No other mammoth here lived that long. Adrian Lister judged one pelvis to be female. That's about it: a handful, out of 61. Three of them also belong to a different species altogether. They're woolly mammoths, the smaller, shaggy cousins from the north.

Which brings us back to the last question. When did all of this happen?

Remember that number, 26,000 years? It was wrong. It came from radiocarbon dating, which works like a clock. Every living thing takes in a special kind of carbon, and after it dies, that carbon slowly runs down. The less that's left, the older the bone. The 26,000-year date stayed the standard figure for decades, even though tests on tooth enamel in 1994 and 2010 came back far older.

The trouble was inside the bones. Radiocarbon dating of bone normally reads a protein called collagen, and these bones didn't have any left. The scientists had to date the bone's mineral, and bone mineral can change. Emma Krolczyk of the US Geological Survey explains that old carbon in the bone can dissolve and be replaced by newer carbon from the groundwater. In her words, "This essentially resets the radiocarbon signal." At the bottom of a spring-fed pond, that swap went on for ages, and the bones tested far younger than they really were.

So the scientists needed a clock the water couldn't reset. In 2016, scientists from the Mammoth Site, the US Geological Survey and the University of Nebraska tried one on the mud itself. It's called luminescence dating. Grains of a common mineral called feldspar slowly store up a tiny signal while they're buried in the dark, and sunlight wipes that signal clean. Measure how much has built up, and you know how long the grains have been underground. They took samples from several depths, and two separate labs ran them.

The results came out on August 11th, 2026, in the journal Quaternary Research. The bones dug up so far were buried between about 160,000 and 232,000 years ago, when our own species was still young and living almost entirely in Africa. The deeper layers are the older ones. Chris Jass, the site's lead scientist, put it simply: the re-dating "suggests that the site is over 100,000 years older than we once thought." Jim Mead, who helped rescue the first bones in 1974, had wanted this answer for a long time. "I have always wanted to get a different chronology method to date the site," he said. Ten years after those first samples, he has his answer.

That changes the scene you've been standing in since the start of this video. The frost wasn't from the last ice age. It was from the one before it. In North America, that earlier cold period is called the Illinoian glaciation, and the front of the great ice sheet came within about 200 miles of this pond.

It changes how long the trap was open, too. The old estimates said a few centuries. Now it looks like tens of thousands of years, and Jass says possibly more than 100,000. Spread across that much time, 61 mammoths is a slow trickle: a young male here, another one centuries later.

Slowly, the pond filled with mud. In its last stage, it was shallow enough for animals to walk in and back out, and the mud from that time is packed with deep footprints and trampled ground. By somewhere between about 73,000 and 61,000 years ago, the pond was full and dry, and the mud in it had hardened almost like cement. The hardened mud was tougher than the soft red rock around it, so as the land slowly wore away, the old pond was left standing as a small hill. Then, one June day in 1974, an earthmover's blade shaved off the top.

Every summer, crews still go down into the dig. In 2023 and 2024 they found part of what's sometimes called an elephant's sixth toe, a small extra bone that helps hold up the foot. A new paper in The Anatomical Record calls it the first ever recorded in an extinct elephant. And they still haven't reached the bottom. The deepest sample dated so far comes from the floor of the dig, but the old sinkhole keeps going down. Jass suspects the deepest dated layers already reach back into a warmer stretch before the Illinoian ice age. Below the floor there's more mud that has never been excavated, and the team guesses it could be 300,000 to 400,000 years old.

Now you're back at the rim, about 170,000 years ago. It's winter, in the ice age before the last one. You're kneeling by the warm water again. The young male is right where we left him, one front foot on the red rock. He shifts his weight onto it. The rock is wet.

Today you can walk around that same pond yourself. There's a roof over it now, and a walkway. Down below, tusks and skulls still lie in the ground where they settled.

Mind the edge.
