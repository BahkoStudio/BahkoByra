## Del 2: Manuset

*2 055 ord, cirka 14 minuter. Snittmening 15,7 ord, längsta 34, noll tankstreck. Skrivs och läses på engelska. CTA:n ingår som sista rad.*

The oldest liquid blood on record came out of the heart of a foal that drowned in mud about 42,000 years ago.

Russian scientists announced it in April 2019, after they cut the foal open. Helping lead that dissection was Hwang Woo-suk, a South Korean scientist whose team made the world's first cloned dog. The plan was to clone the foal, as a first step toward cloning a woolly mammoth. That same week, the scientist who announced it called the blood "absolutely hopeless for cloning purposes."

Seven years later, as far as anyone has reported, the foal is back in a freezer in Yakutsk, with no clone and no living cell to show for it. And nobody has ever published the foal's DNA, so no one can say for certain which horse they were trying to bring back.

In August 2018, four young locals from the village of Boronuk, in Yakutia in northeastern Siberia, spotted a foal, a baby horse, hanging out of a wall of frozen mud. Only two of its four legs were still stuck in the frozen ground. They pulled it free and carried it to an ice cellar.

That wall is the edge of the Batagaika crater. The ground there is permafrost, ground that stays frozen year after year, and as it melts, the hillside sinks and slides away. By 2023 the crater covered about 88 hectares, more than a hundred soccer fields. The media call it the gateway to the underworld. Locals call it Proval, the Collapse. The oldest permafrost in its walls has been frozen for at least 650,000 years.

The same month the foal was found, a Russian and Japanese expedition happened to be at the crater, and North-Eastern Federal University in Yakutsk announced the find. The foal was male, 98 centimeters at the shoulder, about 39 inches, with its skin, its hooves, a mane and a tail. A radiocarbon test done in South Korea later put its age at about 42,000 years. That kind of test reads age from a form of carbon that slowly breaks down.

How old the foal was when it died kept changing. First the team said about three months. Then about two. Then a report said about 20 days. The final answer came months later, from Semyon Grigoriev, head of the university's Mammoth Museum: one to two weeks. It was practically newborn.

It had drowned. The mud it gulped in its last seconds filled its airways and its gut, and then the mud froze around it. "Sadly the gastrointestinal tract was clogged with mud," Grigoriev said later, "so we could not find any mother's milk there."

Hwang's lab had been working toward a find like this for years. In 2012, it signed a deal with the Yakutsk university to try to revive the mammoth. The foal looked like an easier first target. In September 2018, Hwang told The Siberian Times: "If we find only one live cell, we can clone this ancient horse." Cloning the horse, he said, would be "the first step to cloning the mammoth." Grigoriev told the same paper what kind of horse he believed it was: a Lena horse, a wild horse of the Ice Age, "genetically different from those living in Yakutia now."

Hwang's past is mixed. In 2005, his team reported Snuppy, that first cloned dog, and his own university's investigators later confirmed Snuppy was a real clone. In January 2006, two of his papers on cloned human embryos were withdrawn after his university found the data had been made up. In 2009, a court in Seoul convicted him of embezzling research money and of illegally buying human eggs, though it cleared him of the fraud charge.

In February 2019, with Hwang and Grigoriev leading the team, the foal was dissected. "Samples of liquid blood were taken from heart vessels," Grigoriev said. The muscles had kept "their natural reddish colour." There was urine in the bladder, and CT scans, a kind of 3D X-ray, showed a well-preserved brain. Guinness World Records lists the foal's blood as the oldest liquid blood on record, and its urine as the oldest liquid urine.

Normally, Grigoriev told CNN, the blood in Ice Age animals clots, "or even turns to powder," because the fluids slowly dry out over thousands of years, even in permafrost. According to Grigoriev, the only earlier case of liquid blood from the Ice Age was a mammoth his own team found in 2013.

Every one of those details comes from the team itself. As of September 2026, we could find no scientific paper on the foal's blood, its age or its DNA. Nobody has reported how much blood there was.

Grigoriev also told CNN why the blood was hopeless for cloning. To clone an animal, you need a nucleus, the part of a cell that holds almost all of the animal's DNA. And red blood cells, the main cells in blood, "do not have nuclei with DNA."

Cloning works like this. Scientists take an egg, remove its own nucleus, put in the nucleus from the animal they want to copy, and hope the egg starts to divide. If it does, that tiny embryo goes into a living mare, a female horse, who carries it like her own foal. In mammals, red blood cells push their nucleus out before they ever enter the bloodstream. White blood cells do keep theirs. But in dead tissue, every nucleus starts to fall apart. Grigoriev himself said DNA starts to break down soon after death.

So from the start, the team had been searching the muscles and organs for one cell that might have survived. Hwang had left one of his researchers in Yakutsk to try to grow the foal's cells. The method: grind up the tissue, put it in a nutrient broth inside a warm incubator, and check a few days later for any cell that has started to grow. The university sounded confident. In April 2019, its media editor, Michil Yakovlev, was quoted in The Siberian Times: "Hopefully, the world will soon meet the clone of the ancient foal who lived 42,000 years ago." The same paper reported more than 20 attempts in a single month. All of them failed.

Some living things have come back from permafrost. In 2012, scientists writing in the journal PNAS described a flowering plant grown from fruit tissue about 31,800 years old. Eleven years later, a study in PLOS Genetics described tiny roundworms revived after about 46,000 years in a kind of deep sleep. No cell from an Ice Age mammal, the foal included, has been reported to grow again.

The clearest test came from a mammoth. In March 2019, a team at Kindai University in Japan published a study on Yuka, a woolly mammoth found frozen in Yakutia in 2010. Kindai was also a partner on the foal expedition. The Kindai scientists collected 88 nucleus-like structures from the mammoth's muscle and bone marrow and put them into mouse eggs. Some showed signs of activity. None of them divided. The authors pointed to "extensive DNA damage" as a possible cause, and wrote that, with today's methods, cloning a mammoth is impossible in practice. Beth Shapiro, an ancient-DNA scientist then at the University of California, Santa Cruz, put it more simply: "The cells are too damaged."

Now suppose one of the foal's cells had grown anyway. Which horse would they have brought back?

Remember Grigoriev's label, the Lena horse. That label rests on where the foal was found, how long ago it lived, and how it looks. Ludovic Orlando, one of the leading experts on ancient horse DNA, wrote in 2025: "I didn't have the opportunity to work on that 42,000-year-old carcass." So the Lena horse label is still a best guess, and no published test has checked it.

What we know about the Lena horse's DNA comes from other remains, including a stallion about 5,200 years old that came out of the same crater. In 2019, Orlando's team published a study in the journal Cell. It estimated that the Lena horse split off from the ancestors of every living horse roughly 115,000 years ago, and that it passed on no significant share of its DNA to today's domestic horses.

If that label is right, every living horse is about equally distant from the foal on the family tree, including every mare that might carry its clone. In April 2019, the cloning team was choosing between two breeds for that mare. One option was Yakut horses, the shaggy local breed that survives winters in one of the coldest places on Earth. They look like the foal's family. They aren't. In 2015, Orlando's team found that they most likely arrived in Yakutia with the Yakut people, sometime between the years 1200 and 1500. In 800 years or less, about a hundred horse generations at most, they evolved their thick winter coats. Their DNA is "no closer to the extinct population than is any other domesticated horse," Orlando said. The favorite was the other breed: "The Korean horse will fit in perfectly," a Mammoth Museum researcher told The Siberian Times. A Korean horse is a domestic horse too.

Horse cloning itself works. One stallion's clone was born more than 20 years after he died.

The first cloned horse, Prometea, was born in Italy on the 28th of May 2003, carried by the mare she was cloned from. So her mother was also her twin. It took 17 transferred embryos to get her. In 2016, in the final of the Palermo Open, Argentina's top polo tournament, the player Adolfo Cambiaso rode six clones of one mare, Cuartetera, and his team won.

The stallion with the late clone was a Przewalski's horse, an endangered horse from the grasslands of Central Asia. In 1980, the San Diego Zoo's Frozen Zoo, its bank of living animal cells, grew cells from him and froze them. He lived until 1998, but he was allowed to breed in only four of the 21 seasons he could have. Between 1978 and 2023, the breeding program produced more than 8,300 Przewalski's horses. Only 61 of them descend from him.

On the 6th of August 2020, his clone, Kurt, was born in Texas to a quarter horse, a common American breed. The zoo's Bob Wiese said he hoped Kurt would "bring back genetic variation" to the species, meaning a wider mix of genes. A second clone, Ollie, followed in 2023. Across both attempts, 11 embryos led to seven pregnancies and two live foals. Kurt's mitochondrial DNA, a small store of DNA outside the nucleus, came from a domestic horse's egg. Any clone of the foal would have needed an egg from a living horse too.

The cells Kurt came from were alive when they went into the freezer. The foal's cells had been dead for about 42,000 years by the time anyone found them.

The museum named the foal Fuji, after the Japanese TV company that helped organize the expedition. In 2022, it showed Fuji behind glass for about a month. That year, museum researcher Maxim Cheprasov said Hwang was still working on "the possibility of reviving the ancient horse." No result has been announced. Then Fuji went back into the freezer. As far as anyone has reported, that is where it still is.

Meanwhile, the same crater has given Hwang a bigger animal to try. In June 2024, people from Batagay, a town about 7 kilometers away, found a baby mammoth, later named Yana, about 40 meters below the surface. In April 2025, the museum said Hwang, now at a research center in the United Arab Emirates, would study her samples. Cheprasov said what the museum expects: "We realistically do not even hope to find whole cell nuclei in this baby mammoth." Then he added: "But anyway, the samples have been taken."

There is another way to bring back an extinct animal, and it starts with a relative that is still alive. Colossal Biosciences, the company trying to bring back the mammoth, takes cells from a living elephant and edits their DNA. It's taking the same approach with the moa, a giant bird from New Zealand, and Nic Rawlence of the University of Otago has already predicted the likely result: a genetically modified emu.
