## Del 2: Manuset

*2 126 ord, cirka 14 minuter. Snittmening 14,4 ord, längsta 32, noll tankstreck. Skrivs och läses på engelska. CTA:n ingår som sista rad.*

You can hear it before you can see it. A low, throaty sound, from somewhere past the lions and the polar bears. It's the last week of August, 1936, in Hobart. You've come on the double-decker tram and paid sixpence at the zoo gate. For days now, the afternoons haven't climbed past ten degrees Celsius.

Then you find it. A small enclosure with a concrete floor, and inside, one animal walking round and round. It's the size of a medium dog, with a long stiff tail and dark stripes running across its back and rump. It stops, looks at you, and opens its jaws in an enormous yawn.

It's alone. And within two weeks, it will be dead.

This is the last known thylacine, the animal most people call the Tasmanian tiger. And the island around it is a place where, within living memory, you could be paid a pound for killing one.

So what was it like to live in Tasmania while a whole species came down to one known animal, pacing a concrete floor in a city zoo?

Let's start with what we know for certain.

It died on the evening of Monday the 7th of September, 1936. Nine days later, the council committee that ran Hobart's parks and zoo wrote it into the official record of its meeting. The tiger, it said, "died on Monday evening last," and "the body has been forwarded to the Museum."

On the 10th of July, 1936, Tasmania had finally made the thylacine a fully protected species, so killing one was now against the law. That was 59 days before the last known one died.

Almost everything else about this animal is argued over. Two teams of researchers can't even agree whether it was male or female. We'll come back to that. And the name you may have heard, Benjamin, is a myth. It first appeared in 1968, in a story told by a man who claimed to have worked at the zoo. The researcher Robert Paddle found no zoo record that he ever worked there.

The road to that concrete floor starts with sheep. In 1830, the Van Diemen's Land Company, a sheep-farming business in the northwest, offered its first reward for a dead thylacine. Van Diemen's Land was Tasmania's old name. In 1886, a member of parliament named John Lyne claimed that "30,000 or 40,000 sheep were killed annually," and asked for money to wipe out the tigers. The vote was tied, ten to ten. Following custom, the Speaker broke the tie with a yes, so the idea could be debated further. Two years later, the bounty, a government reward for every dead thylacine, became law.

From 1888 to 1909, the government paid £1 for every adult thylacine killed and ten shillings, half a pound, for every young one. By the time the scheme ended, it had paid out 2,184 bounties. The National Museum of Australia estimates that at least 3,500 thylacines were killed by people between 1830 and the 1920s.

Now, some of you are thinking: surely the farmers had a point. It was a sheep killer, wasn't it?

Fair question. It came up even in 1886. In that same debate, another member asked "whether this animal killed sheep" at all, and Lyne had to assure parliament that it did.

The bones help answer it. In 2020, a team led by Douglas Rovinsky worked out that an average thylacine weighed about 16.7 kilograms, around 37 pounds. The old estimate had been about 30 kilograms. Then in August 2026, Vera Weisbecker's team studied its skull. Its jaws were built for fast, snapping bites, and were "probably unsuited to holding down large animals like sheep." Hobart's newspaper The Mercury had described its bite as "a series of snaps" as early as 1884.

Still, some thylacines did kill sheep. In 1905, The Examiner in Launceston reported a large female "surprised killing a sheep in the day time." Some sheep, yes. A plague, no. The National Museum of Australia says wild and stray dogs, and bad management caused most of the losses, and the thylacine became "an easy scapegoat."

So let's rebuild one winter day in the Tasmanian bush, in the years when the last wild thylacines were still out there. The order is our guess, but every detail comes from the historical record.

It's early morning in the highlands, and you're walking a snare line. You and your partner have set as many as a thousand snares this season, loops of hemp cord that catch an animal by the leg. You check them from a rough camp in the bush, and you only get back to your main camp every three days.

By midday, you're skinning the catch, and the catch is money. Back in 1925, a possum skin fetched almost 18 shillings, close to the pound the government once paid for a dead tiger. Now the Great Depression has hit. Nearly a third of Tasmania's workers are out of a job, and skins are cash when little else is. In the 1934 season alone, trappers take nearly a million and a half ringtail possums.

In the evening, you're back at camp. Another trapper out here, Elias Churchill, keeps a hut on the track to a mining camp called Adamsfield. He says tigers are shy and nervous. Camp near one, and by nightfall it's "five miles away."

Somewhere out there in the dark, a thylacine is hunting, alone or with one other. It's after wallabies, small cousins of the kangaroo, and smaller animals, and it wants its kill fresh. It almost never comes back to an old carcass, so poison baits hardly ever work on it. In 1863, the naturalist Ronald Gunn wrote that people had found it "impossible to kill them by means of strychnine," a common poison. Your snares are set for wallabies, but they catch whatever steps into them.

By the 1930s, there were very few left. In 1937, a hunter named Luke Etchell said that years earlier he had caught "six or seven Native Tigers during a hunting season." But in the years after that, he hadn't seen a single one.

The last one known to have been killed in the wild was shot at midday on the 13th of May, 1930, near a place called Mawbanna in the northwest. A farmer's son named Wilf Batty shot it by the family's poultry shed after his sister spotted it. The local paper's headline called it a "hyena," another old nickname for the thylacine. Many retellings give the date as the 6th of May. Newspaper records from the time point to the 13th.

Live ones still turned up now and then, caught in snares. Churchill described one capture in a 1957 magazine interview. The animal was caught by the foot. He slipped a snare around its jaws, pulled it tight, and carried the animal back to camp slung around his neck. "It weighed the best part of forty pounds," he said, nearly 18 kilograms. "It didn't seem to have any spirit at all." In the log pen he built for it, the article says, "for hours on end it kept pacing around the fence, a habit also noted later when it was in the zoo."

And in Hobart, anyone with sixpence could see one up close.

In December 1933, a young naturalist named David Fleay filmed a thylacine at the zoo. His footage runs just under 80 seconds, the longest film of a thylacine that exists. In it, the animal lies down, paces the edge of its small enclosure, sniffs the air, scratches, and opens its jaws in a wide yawn it used as a warning. Fleay was bitten on the buttocks after he finished. Another clip, just 21 seconds long, was shot for a tourist film called Tasmania the Wonderland in 1935. It's the last known moving footage of a living thylacine.

So which one was pacing that floor in August 1936? In 2023, two teams published different answers. Robert Paddle and museum curator Kathryn Medlock, working with the Tasmanian Museum and Art Gallery, say it was an old female that Elias Churchill sold to the zoo in May 1936. Gareth Linnard and Stephen Sleightholme say it was the male in Fleay's film, caught as a youngster in 1930 and kept at the zoo for six years. Churchill said he caught two tigers alive, so the snare story he told in 1957 may be about a different animal. Both teams agree on the night it died.

Two people who heard thylacines at this zoo left us a description of the sound. One was Alison Reid. Her father, Arthur, ran the zoo until he died in December 1935. In 1968, she wrote to The Mercury about the times the thylacines had been "left shut out from their sleeping quarters." They would run around in a panic, she wrote, "uttering this 'coughing bark,'" until someone came and opened the door. The other, Peggie Bassett, visited the zoo as a small child. In 2025, at 94, she told the ABC, Australia's national broadcaster: "Before you actually got there you could hear it." It was, she said, "not barking or howling, just a low guttural sort of sound."

Which brings us back to that cold week in August.

Weather records from a Hobart weather station a couple of kilometers from the zoo show a real cold snap in late August 1936. For a week, afternoon highs stayed between about 8 and 10 degrees Celsius, and on the morning of the 31st the temperature dropped to just above freezing. After the death, The Mercury reported that the tiger had been in good health but "contracted a chill during the recent spell of cold weather."

A lot of videos say it froze to death on its last night. But by the 7th of September, the cold spell was over. The night before, the same station recorded a low of about 5 degrees. That Monday afternoon reached about 17 degrees, the warmest since the cold snap began. Robert Paddle believes it had been shut out of its sleeping den and died of exposure. Linnard and Sleightholme say nothing written at the time supports that. The records show a cold week, a chill, and an animal that died on a mild Monday evening.

The same council notes that recorded the death added one more line: "Efforts to be made by Superintendent to obtain another tiger up to the value of £30 each." The £30 tiger never came. In February 1937, The Examiner asked its readers: "Has anybody seen a Tasmanian tiger lately?" The state's wildlife board was about to ask all of Tasmania the same thing. By the end of that year, the zoo itself had closed.

People have kept reporting sightings ever since. A 2023 study by Barry Brook and colleagues gathered 1,237 records from 1910 onward. Counting only the most reliable ones, the study puts extinction sometime between the 1940s and the 1970s. If you count every sighting, thylacines may have lasted into the late 1980s or later, with a small chance that a few survived even longer in the remote southwest. Since 1936, nobody has produced a body or a confirmed photo.

Today, a company called Colossal Biosciences is trying to bring the thylacine back. Andrew Pask is the lead geneticist on the project at Melbourne University, which has teamed up with Colossal. In September 2026, he said he is aiming for a living thylacine by 2036, a hundred years after the last known one died. Two years earlier, Kris Helgen of the Australian Museum had been blunt. A dunnart is a small, mouse-sized animal with a pouch, and he said: "All that's being promised is a genetically modified dunnart."

And the zoo's last thylacine? For decades, nobody knew which of the museum's specimens was its body. In 2022, Robert Paddle and Kathryn Medlock found a skin and skeleton in a cupboard there. They believe it's the zoo's last thylacine, the old female Churchill sold in May 1936. The skin had been used in school visits, and it had flattened patches where children had been allowed to pat it.

Now go back to the zoo. It's the last week of August, 1936, and once again you hear it before you see it. You're standing at the enclosure, looking at the concrete floor, with the cold coming through your coat. The thylacine walks one more lap, then another. On the island around you, killing one has just become illegal. Nobody here knows yet that this is the last one anyone will ever be sure of.

It keeps walking.
