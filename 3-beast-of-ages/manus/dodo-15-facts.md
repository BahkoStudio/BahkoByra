## Del 2: Manuset

On October 14, 2026, Colossal Biosciences, the Texas company trying to bring back the dodo, said its AI had flagged about 2,300 genetic switches for the bird's beak. Those are bits of DNA that turn genes on and off. TIME wrote that a living dodo could come in the early 2030s, though Colossal itself gave no date that day. Yet only one dodo anywhere still has flesh on it, in Oxford, and it was shot in the back of the head. Here are fifteen facts about the dodo that make no sense. We end with the one question nobody can settle: when the last dodo died.

Fact one. The last dodo flesh on Earth has lead shot in it. What survives is a skull with skin still on it, the ring of bone around the eye, a thigh bone, a foot and one feather. In 2018, a University of Warwick team led by Professor Mark Williams scanned the Oxford Dodo's head with micro-CT, a scanner that stacks X-rays into a 3D picture. They found pellets in the back of the head and in the neck. The skull was so thick that the shot never got through. The pellets were the kind typically used in the 1600s to hunt wildfowl, meaning ducks and geese. Professor Paul Smith of Oxford said, "we thought the bird had come to the museum after being displayed as a live specimen in London." The pellets cast doubt on that story.

Fact two. The famous rescue from the fire probably never happened. The story goes that in 1755 the rotting stuffed dodo was thrown on a fire, and a curator snatched the head and foot from the flames. Guinness World Records calls that a legend, now debunked. A record from 1755 does say the university's vice-chancellor and the trustees ordered the decaying bird destroyed. Yet the head and foot show no sign of fire, which suggests someone simply kept what could be saved. One explanation, passed on in 2013 by Jack Ashby of the Grant Museum at University College London, comes down to a misread word. A Latin word for "inspection" looks a lot like the Latin word for "fire," so a note about inspecting the bird may have been read as burning it.

Fact three. Every letter of dodo DNA that science has read, its full set of genes, comes from a single bird. In March 2022, the geneticist Beth Shapiro told a Royal Society webinar that her team had read it from a Danish specimen. The Oxford head had too little well-preserved DNA. Colossal has exactly one dodo genome. For its woolly mammoth project, it has about a hundred. Cold preserves DNA better than the tropical heat of Mauritius does. On October 14, 2026, Colossal said about 95 percent of the genome had been read at least five times over. The whole code is about two billion letters long. Shapiro told TIME, "We're really not missing much of the dodo genome." The genome study is still being checked by other scientists. So the most famous extinct bird on Earth has one DNA blueprint, and it comes from Denmark.

Fact four. Science first filed the dodo with the ostriches. In 1758, Carl Linnaeus named it Struthio cucullatus, putting it in the same group as ostriches. Cucullatus means hooded. Two years later, the French zoologist Brisson called it Raphus. Then in 1766, Linnaeus renamed it Didus ineptus. That makes three scientific names in eight years. By the early 1800s, some considered the dodo and its cousin from Rodrigues, the solitaire, to be "mythological beasts," until Victorian scientists confirmed they were real. In 1842, the Danish zoologist Reinhardt proposed what they actually were: ground pigeons.

Fact five. The dodo split from its closest cousin before its island even existed. That cousin was the solitaire, the big flightless bird of Rodrigues. DNA work in 2002 put their split at more than twenty million years ago. Mauritius is less than ten million years old. Rodrigues is about one and a half million. The geneticist Alan Cooper said the result "suggests that the solitaire, at least, may have still been able to fly as recently as 1.5 million years ago." Scientists have suggested other islands in the region worked as stepping stones. DNA points to the Nicobar pigeon as the dodo's closest living relative, and it still flies between islands today.

Fact six. The dodo was probably fast. "Evidence from bone specimens suggests that the Dodo's tendon which closed its toes was exceptionally powerful," said the palaeontologist Neil Gostling of the University of Southampton. "The dodo was almost certainly a very active, very fast animal." His colleague Mark Young added that the few written accounts describe "a fast-moving animal that loved the forest." Their 2024 review of 400 years of writing and museum collections also found that only two species of these giant pigeons ever existed, the dodo and the solitaire. Earlier biologists had proposed as many as five. Other bone studies put the dodo at about 10 to 14 kilograms, close to a large wild turkey. Neither the dodo nor the solitaire ever got a type specimen, the one official example a species is supposed to have. According to the team, they vanished too fast.

Fact seven. The dodo had a pigeon's brain and a sense of smell unusual for a bird. In a study published in 2016, Maria Eugenia Leone Gold and her colleagues CT-scanned a dodo skull at the Natural History Museum in London. They built a 3D model of the space where the brain sat. Gold said the brain was "exactly the size you would predict it to be for its body size," and that dodos "probably had a similar intelligence level to pigeons." The surprise was at the front of the brain. The parts that handle smell were enlarged. Most birds rely on sight instead. The dodo probably sniffed out fruit, small animals and shellfish.

Fact eight. Its bones kept a calendar. In 2017, a team including Julian Hume published the first study of thin slices of dodo bone under a microscope. "We propose that the dodo bred around August," they wrote. The chicks then raced to grow big and strong before the cyclone season, from November to March, when food was short. Then, from March to the end of July, the adults moulted, shedding and regrowing their feathers. Sailors had described dodos with downy feathers, with black down, or with grey curled tail feathers. Those clashing reports fit birds seen at different points of that moult. Two of the bones even held medullary bone, a layer females build when making eggs. In the team's words, it "proves unequivocally" that some were females getting ready to lay.

Fact nine. The Dutch named it after its taste, and still hunted it. The name Walghvoghel first appears in the journal of Vice-Admiral Wybrand van Warwijck, who visited Mauritius in 1598. It means, roughly, the tasteless bird. The original Dutch report is lost, but an English translation survives. It says the longer the bird was cooked, the worse it got. "Nevertheless their belly and breast were of a pleasant flavour and easily masticated," meaning easy to chew. Some hunters killed dodos only for the gizzard, the muscly part of the stomach that grinds food. Dodos swallowed stones to help the gizzard do that. Around 1638, Sir Hamon L'Estrange paid to see a live dodo in London. The keeper fed it pebbles, and it swallowed them. Later, in L'Estrange's words, it "cast them all againe," bringing every one back up.

Fact ten. A dodo made it to Japan. Dutch documents show that a live dodo was shipped in 1647, by way of Batavia, today's Jakarta, to Dejima, a Dutch trading island at Nagasaki. It arrived alive. Julian Hume and Ria Winters published the records in 2014, and that bird is the last known captive dodo. At least eleven dodos reached foreign ports alive. At least two survived a journey to India in 1625.

Fact eleven. The best picture of a living dodo may have been painted in India. It came from the court of the Mughal emperor Jahangir in the early 1600s, and it is attributed to the court painter Ustad Mansur. Julian Hume and the scholar A. Iwanow regard it as one of the most accurate depictions of a living dodo. The bird may have been one of the two live dodos the English traveller Peter Mundy described at Surat, a port in western India. The painting turned up in a Russian collection in the 1950s. Meanwhile, in Europe, Roelant Savery's painting of 1626 became the standard image of the fat dodo.

Fact twelve. Only one dodo skeleton in the world comes from a single bird. It is known as the Thirioux skeleton, after Etienne Thirioux, and it stands in Port Louis, the capital of Mauritius. A 2016 study calls it "the only known complete dodo skeleton, and the only one comprising the bones of a single individual." The author Errol Fuller said there are only twelve nearly complete dodo skeletons in the world. The others are composites, built from the bones of more than one bird. Almost all the dodo bones in museums come from one swamp, the Mare aux Songes. In November 2016, one almost complete composite came up at auction in West Sussex, in England. A private bidder on the phone paid about three hundred and forty thousand British pounds.

Fact thirteen. The white dodo of Réunion was an ibis. Around 1611, in Prague, Roelant Savery painted a whitish Mauritius dodo. Other painters copied it. In the 1800s, those paintings surfaced and were linked to travellers' tales of a white bird on Réunion, the island next to Mauritius, that they called a "solitaire," so a Réunion white dodo entered the books. In 1974, the first ibis bones were dug up on Réunion, from a wading bird with a long curved bill. They showed that white bird had been an ibis all along. In 2004, Julian Hume and Anthony Cheke traced the white paintings back to Savery's pale Mauritius dodo.

Fact fourteen. Colossal's scientists are already changing bird faces inside living embryos. Back to the AI from the start. Colossal calls it GenoMorph. It was built from embryo samples of chickens, quail, ducks and pigeons, plus skull scans of more than 70 bird species. Its roughly 2,300 switches are only candidates, still to be tested. Ben Lamm, Colossal's chief executive, called it a "treasure map for the dodo genome." He also said, "Identifying a potential edit doesn't mean we immediately introduce it into an animal or assume it's safe." To hatch a dodo, Colossal plans to use chicken eggs, because Nicobar pigeons, the bird DNA points to as the dodo's closest living relative, lay so few. Then the strange part. In a study other scientists have not yet reviewed, Colossal describes using tiny fat particles to carry gene-editing tools into bird embryos. Editing a bone gene called Runx2 clearly changed the face and skull. The same delivery system also worked in embryos of ducks and of rock doves, the common city pigeon.

Fact fifteen. Nobody knows when the last dodo died. The last confirmed sighting was in 1662, by Volkert Evertsz, a survivor of the wrecked Dutch ship Arnhem, on a small island off Mauritius. A statistical study in the journal Nature put the end at around 1690. Then in 2004, Hume and colleagues went through hunting records kept by Isaac Lamotius, head of the Dutch base on the island. Those records list dodos being caught for at least 26 years after 1662. In 2020, Anthony Cheke and Jolyon Parish argued those later dodos were more likely red hens, a different flightless bird. In their words, folklore "preserved the name and transferred it to the red hen." The red hen itself was last mentioned in 1693.

That shot head is still in Oxford. Neil Gostling called the dodo "the first living thing that was recorded as being present and then disappeared." Nobody can say what year the last one died. Colossal is betting that someone will get to write down the year the next one hatches. The same company is also working to bring back the Tasmanian tiger.
