In a lab in Texas, a team just hatched twenty-six chicks inside shells that no bird ever laid. They built that technology for one reason: to help bring back a bird that once stood twelve feet tall. So how much of what we think we know about that bird actually holds up under a closer look? Almost none of it. Here are fifteen facts about the moa that make no sense, ending with the one fact that might decide whether the animal Colossal eventually produces gets to be called a moa at all.

Start with how anyone even knew the moa existed. In 1839, the British anatomist Richard Owen was handed a single fragment of thighbone, six inches long, that had arrived from New Zealand with no explanation attached. From that one piece of bone, Owen announced that an enormous, flightless, ostrich-like bird had once lived there. Full skeletons arrived from New Zealand three years later, in 1842, and matched his prediction bone for bone. Owen named the genus Dinornis in 1843, built almost entirely from a single, correct guess.

What those skeletons showed was stranger than Owen could have expected. Every flightless bird alive today still carries some leftover wing bones. The ostrich has them. So does the emu, the cassowary, even the small, round kiwi. A body part rarely disappears completely once a lineage stops using it; some stub or splint usually survives out of habit. The moa broke that rule completely. Of every bird species ever studied, living or extinct, it is the only one known to have lost its wing bones completely. There is nothing where wings should be, not even a trace.

The rest of the skeleton matched that strangeness. Like every ratite, the moa had a flat sternum with no keel, since a keel exists to anchor flight muscles, and the moa kept no flight muscles to anchor. What it built was a body organized almost entirely around its legs: leg joints as tall as an adult human's knee, a neck stretching past a meter, and a head so small it looks borrowed from a different animal. Nine separate species carried this basic plan, ranging from something turkey-sized up to Dinornis, the giant that reached 3.6 meters.

The genus Owen named would go on to fool his successors for more than a century and a half, because in Dinornis, the two sexes barely look like the same animal. Females weren't just larger than males; they reached up to 150 percent of a male's height and 280 percent of his weight. Males topped out around 85 kilograms. Females reached 240. No other bird on record shows a size gap between the sexes that extreme, and it was wide enough to convince generations of naturalists that a single species was actually three. One of those supposed species got its own name, the "slender moa," for bones once judged too delicate to belong with the giants. DNA testing in the early 2000s identified every single slender moa specimen as male. An entire species had been invented by mistake, because nobody expected a male and a female of the same bird to look that different from each other.

Which living bird is the moa actually related to? Most people guess the emu, given the similar size and the shared inability to fly. Genetic sequencing points somewhere else: the tinamou, a small, weak-flying bird from Central and South America, light enough to perch on a forearm.

Geography fails again on the other side of the planet. The elephant bird of Madagascar, a giant flightless bird in its own right, turns out to be closest kin to the kiwi, New Zealand's small, ground-dwelling native bird, despite the two looking nothing alike and living oceans apart.

For as long as the moa lineage existed, nothing on the ground could kill an adult. Its one real predator lived in the air: Haast's eagle, a raptor with a wingspan approaching three meters, heavy enough to strike a moa at speed and use its talons like a pair of axes, built specifically to take down birds many times its own size. Everything the moa had ever needed to fear came from above.

People changed that arithmetic almost overnight. Human settlers reached New Zealand around 1280 AD, and within roughly a hundred to two hundred years, every one of the nine moa species was gone. A 2019 PNAS chronology of Māori settlement estimates the hunting population never exceeded a couple of thousand people, smaller than a single rural town. A bird with no ground predator in its entire history was erased by a group of humans who simply found it easier to catch than to fear. Haast's eagle went down with it. Once its main prey was gone, that eagle vanished within roughly the same window of time.

The forest never fully let go of that memory. Many of New Zealand's native shrubs still grow in a strange, tangled form called divaricating: densely interlaced stems, wide branching angles, and leaves too small for a large browsing bird to bother with. This shape may have evolved specifically to survive moa browsing, a decades-old hypothesis that feeding trials tend to support: both birds and hoofed animals tend to avoid these plants whenever anything softer is available nearby. Some New Zealand forests may still be armored against a browser that has been gone for six hundred years.

The eggs come with their own contradiction. The largest known moa egg measured 240 by 178 millimeters, holding roughly eighty times the volume of a chicken egg. The shell itself ran only about 1.2 to 1.4 millimeters thick, thin for an egg that size. Of more than 3,400 bird species ever tested for shell strength, living and extinct, the moa's egg came out the most fragile of all of them. The largest bird in New Zealand's history entrusted its future to one of the flimsiest containers in the entire animal kingdom.

Some of that history survived in a form that shouldn't exist after centuries underground. The dry, cold caves of Central Otago mummified some moa remains, preserving tissue and skin in a way ordinary fossils never do. A foot recovered from Mount Owen still carried scaly skin, muscle, and tendon, roughly 3,300 years after the bird died. Near Waikaia, a leg turned up with its ligaments and feathers still attached down to the ankle, skin included, the mottled pattern visible on the plumage. These are physical pieces of an animal that vanished centuries before anyone thought to record what a living one looked like.

Which brings the story back to Texas. In May 2026, Colossal Biosciences announced a breakthrough aimed at solving the fragile-egg problem directly: a 3D-printed silicone lattice that functions as an artificial eggshell, its membrane engineered to let oxygen pass through evenly without any external tubing, and reportedly able to be manufactured at any size. As proof of concept, the company hatched twenty-six ordinary chicks from it. Andrew Pask, Colossal's chief biological officer, has explained why the technology needed to exist at all: no living bird species is large enough to sit on, or hatch, an egg the size a moa would lay. The project runs in partnership with the Ngāi Tahu Research Centre and Canterbury Museum, funded in part by filmmaker Peter Jackson, with the stated goal of restoring the South Island giant moa specifically. If a moa hatches again, it will do so inside a machine, since nothing alive today is built to be its mother.

Here's the fact that undercuts the whole plan. Bringing a moa back requires editing a large bird's genome into something resembling a moa's. Nic Rawlence, a paleogeneticist at the University of Otago, has pointed out the problem with that plan: the moa's actual closest relative, the tinamou, is far too small to donate a genome for, leaving the project to work with the emu, the exact bird an earlier fact already showed isn't really family at all. Rawlence has predicted the project's likely result: a genetically modified emu wearing a moa's name.

Philip Seddon, a professor of zoology at the same university, raises a separate objection, stating plainly that no genetic engineering pathway currently exists that can truly bring back a species that has gone extinct.

Richard Owen built an entire, correct science out of a single bone in 1839. More than a century and a half later, his own genus fooled the scientists who followed him, splitting one moa into three species because its body misled them about what it actually was. Now, in the attempt to bring the moa back, a similar kind of mistaken identity may be happening again, deliberately this time: a bird assembled from the wrong relative, wearing something close to the right shape, hatching inside a Texas machine that has already kept twenty-six chicks alive inside shells no bird laid. What eventually walks out of that machine may well share the moa's height. Whether it gets to share the moa's name is a question no gene-editing lab has settled yet.

It also isn't the only Colossal project where the wrong relative ended up deciding what came out the other end.
