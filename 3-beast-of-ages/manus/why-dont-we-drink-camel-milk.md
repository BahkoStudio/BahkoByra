On a farm in Missouri, an Amish dairyman is milking a camel by hand before sunrise. The milk will be pasteurized and bottled for a company called Desert Farms. It will end up on a shelf at a Whole Foods, lost among rows of cow milk nobody thinks twice about. So why don't we drink camel milk in the West?

Your own guess is probably that nobody ever tried to sell it here. That guess is wrong. Desert Farms has sold camel milk in America since 2014. It comes raw or pasteurized, and also as a kefir-style drink. Around a hundred and fifty stores carry it today, Whole Foods among them. A decade of trying has still left it a niche product. A second guess is that it tastes wrong to Western palates. That guess fails too. It mostly tastes close to cow milk, just with a faint salty edge. The real answer starts with what happens when you try to turn camel milk into cheese.

Cow milk turns into cheese because of a protein called kappa-casein. Rennet, the enzyme cheesemakers add to milk, cuts that protein into pieces, and the pieces clump together into curds. Cow milk usually runs about thirteen percent kappa-casein. Camel milk carries far less on average, though the amount swings wildly from camel to camel, and a few samples even beat the cow. Rennet on its own mostly fails, and camel milk barely thickens. The same gap runs the other way too. Camel milk carries almost none of the beta-lactoglobulin that makes up most of the whey in cow milk. That protein sits behind roughly eight in ten human milk allergies. For most of history, before refrigeration, cheese served one purpose. It let milk survive the weeks between one milking and the next. A milk that resists turning into cheese spoils fast, in places already hard on stored food.

There is a place where someone solved that problem anyway. In 1987, a woman named Nancy Abeiderrahmane opened a dairy in Mauritania and set out to make camel cheese despite the missing protein. It took five years of failed batches. In 1992, a food scientist from the United Nations agency for agriculture taught her a workaround. The fix raises the milk's acidity and adds a mineral salt, and either one alone leaves the cheese too soft to sell. Together they let a different enzyme finish the job kappa-casein usually does. A simpler shortcut works too: mixing camel milk with cow or buffalo milk lets ordinary rennet get a grip. The result, Caravane, became the world's first commercial camel cheese to reach a real market, a soft white wheel that tastes close to brie. Her dairy, Tiviski, still sells it today in the capital, Nouakchott, and across the border in Senegal. It also bottles zrig, a soured camel milk drink nomads have made for generations without rennet at all.

That number matters more once you set it beside a bigger one. Camel milk production has grown steadily worldwide. It rose from about three and a half million tons in 2014 to somewhere past four million tons today. A joint estimate from the United Nations and French agricultural researchers puts the real number as high as five million. World milk production passed nine hundred and sixty five million tons in 2023. Cow milk alone makes up about eighty one percent of that. Camel milk is a rounding error next to it, a fraction of one percent of the milk humans drink each year.

The reason runs deeper than cheese, and it starts with geography. Camels were domesticated on the Arabian Peninsula, somewhere around four thousand years ago, in what is now Oman and Saudi Arabia. Wide feet and a fat-storing hump instead of thick fur let it thrive where cattle cannot. A three-chambered stomach lets it live on thorny, salt-tolerant plants that would leave a dairy cow hungry within weeks. The wild ancestor of today's camel died out roughly two thousand years after people first domesticated it. Cattle farming later spread into a wetter, colder Europe. By then, camels were already a purely Arabian animal, with no wild population anywhere nearby to draw a European farmer's attention.

Europe knew the tame camel well all the same. Emperor Trajan raised a thousand-strong camel cavalry unit and stationed it on the empire's Syrian frontier for two centuries. Every one of those camels lived out its life on desert duty, thousands of kilometers from the nearest Roman dairy.

America came closer than Rome ever did, and it had nothing to do with milk. In 1853, Jefferson Davis became United States Secretary of War, years before he would lead the Confederacy. The Army had just taken vast stretches of desert from Mexico, and mules were dying of thirst on routes camels crossed easily. In 1855, Congress gave Davis thirty thousand dollars to test the idea. Soldiers bought camels around the Mediterranean, from Tunisia to Turkey. More purchases brought the total to seventy by 1857, based at a post in Texas called Camp Verde. That same year, an officer named Edward Beale took twenty five of the camels on a survey through northern Arizona. The trip lasted forty eight days and cut a wagon road toward the Colorado River, a route that later became part of Route 66. Beale reported that the camels hauled water for his mules and crossed sharp rock without injury.

Horses and mules were the real trouble. The scent of a camel made every one of them bolt. The soldiers assigned to handle the camels grew to hate the smell just as much. Then the Civil War arrived, and Jefferson Davis left Washington to lead the Confederacy. The men still running the Union Army had little interest in reviving a program built by the enemy's new president. The corps was disbanded within a few years, and the camels were sold at auction. Nearly forty of them escaped custody at Camp Verde just before the fighting began.

Those forty camels kept crossing the same stretches of desert for decades, long after the last soldier who rode one was gone. One legend even grew out of it. Ranchers near the Arizona border described a huge red camel with what looked like a corpse lashed to its back. Nobody could identify the body, and the straps had scarred deep enough to reach bone. It trampled a woman to death in 1883, and a farmer finally shot it a decade later in his own garden. Real sightings of ordinary camels lasted into the 1890s, though accounts of the very last one disagree by decades. One of them, a female named Topsy, ended up working mines and touring with a circus before living out her final years at the Los Angeles Zoo. They spent their whole lives as pack animals or, later, as escapees turned local curiosities. Not one of them ever produced a drop of milk anyone thought to collect.

Here is the uncomfortable part. The camels carried everything asked of them, on far less water than any mule needed. A war interrupted the program right as it was proving itself. The herd went to auction before anyone thought to build something more lasting around it. A dairy industry needs a population of animals to draw from, year after year. Once the corps was sold off, America simply had one working animal fewer, and nothing left to build a tradition on.

The West's one real shot at a camel milk tradition came and went inside someone else's war, before it ever properly started.

Which brings it back to that Missouri farm before sunrise. The Amish family milking their camel today is building a tradition from nothing. They stand on the same ground where seventy working camels once crossed deserts no mule could survive.
