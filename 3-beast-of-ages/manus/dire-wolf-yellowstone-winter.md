## Del 2: Manuset

Two years ago, on the first of October, dogs gave birth to two white wolf pups. Today Romulus weighs 134 pounds. His brother Remus weighs 132. Their sister Khaleesi, a few months younger, weighs 102. They live on a private preserve of more than 2,000 acres, and the company that made them, Colossal Biosciences, calls them dire wolves. Colossal says it has no plans to release them into the wild.

So we're going to do it here, once, on paper. The first of November. The Lamar Valley, Yellowstone. The gate opens, and three white wolves step into snow they have never smelled. Each of you gets a clock. It runs until April, and only one of you will still be running when it stops. At the last count, 84 wild wolves lived in this park. Their ancestors arrived the same way you do, out of a gate. In 1995 and 1996, wolves from Canada were let go here, 41 in all, about seventy years after the last known wolf pack in Yellowstone was killed.

The release is made up, and so is everything that happens to these three after it. The wolves, the park and every way a wolf dies here are real.

Before the gate opens, you should know what's walking out.

The real dire wolf was not a big gray wolf. In 2021 a team led by Angela Perri read its DNA and published it in the journal Nature. Dire wolves split from the line that led to gray wolves nearly six million years ago. And their genes show no trace of them ever mating with gray wolves or coyotes.

Colossal's three are something else. They started as gray wolf cells. The company made 20 small changes to their DNA, spread across 14 genes. Then dogs carried the pups as surrogate mothers. Even the white coat comes from gray wolf genes, because copying the dire wolf's own version risked harming the pups. In April 2025, a group of canine experts who advise the IUCN, the world's main conservation body, said these animals should not be called dire wolves. The scientist Jacquelyn Gill, who studies ice age animals, called them genetically modified gray wolves.

Keep that in your head. By April, it decides who wins.

Almost every dire wolf fossil ever found comes from well south of here. Yellowstone sits farther north than nearly all of them. A handful of bones have been claimed further north, and none of them is settled. The real dire wolf may never have walked this valley at all.

Run one.

You're Romulus, the biggest of the three. When you were eight months old, Colossal called you "ever the front-runner." You've eaten meat every day of your life. Somebody else always put it there.

The first thing you notice in the Lamar Valley is the smell. Every few hundred yards, on a rock, a post, a clump of sage, another wolf has left a message. Most of those 84 wolves live in seven packs. Here in the north of the park, where you are, two of those families had sixteen wolves each at the last count. They're called 8 Mile and Rescue Creek.

You are three. They are sixteen.

Yellowstone's biologists have spent years recording what happens when two packs meet. The side with more wolves usually wins, and packs with older wolves do better too. You have no numbers and no experience. What you have is weight.

Day four, you find the elk, and there are fewer than you'd think. The elk of northern Yellowstone don't stay in the park for winter. They walk downhill toward the farms and the town of Gardiner, where the snow is thinner. In April 2025, Montana counted about 7,200 elk in that herd from a helicopter. More than 6,000 of them were standing outside the park.

So the food is leaving, and you stay where the smells are.

Day eleven, you get lucky. A cow elk is down in a creek bed, old and thin, and the three of you pull her out together. You've hunted as a pack before, behind a fence. Out here there's no fence and nobody coming with a bucket. You eat until you can't move.

That's the problem. A kill in the open doesn't stay secret. By morning the ravens are on it, and a cloud of ravens can be seen for miles.

Inside this park, the most common natural way for a wolf to die is being killed by other wolves, mostly from rival packs. A wolf in Yellowstone lives four to five years on average.

Day twenty-three. You're back at the creek bed, cracking the last of the bones, when the howl comes from the ridge. Then another. Then too many to count.

You were bred to be big, and you are. You've also never been in a fight where the other side was trying to kill you.

You don't run. Front-runners don't.

Romulus. Twenty-three days. Beaten by a family of sixteen.

Twenty-three days is the number to beat. And the smallest of the three watched the whole fight. If you're enjoying this, subscribe. Now, run two.

You're Khaleesi. You saw what happened at the creek bed from a distance, and you learned one thing from it. Don't stay where the packs are.

So you follow the elk.

It works. You go north and downhill, the way the herd goes every winter. The snow gets thinner. The elk get thicker. Every few days there's a weak one in the drifts. You start to put weight back on.

Park biologists know this path well. The wolves in the north of the park spend about five percent of their time outside Yellowstone, most of it in late fall, following the same animals you are.

And the park ends at a line. On a map it's a straight edge. On the ground, nothing marks it. A wolf can't read it.

Day thirty, you cross it for the first time. You don't notice.

On the other side, wolves are not protected the same way. In Montana, wolf hunting is legal in season. In the winter of 2021 to 2022, hunters killed about 25 Yellowstone wolves that had stepped out of the park. That's roughly one in five of the park's wolves. One pack, Junction Butte, lost three in the first week alone.

It still happens. In 2025, two of the park's wolves with tracking collars were shot legally in Montana.

Yellowstone has been through this with the most famous wolf it ever had. She was born in Yellowstone in 2006, and researchers knew her as O-Six. She led the Lamar Canyon pack, and visitors lined the road to watch her through spotting scopes. In December 2012 she left the park, and a hunter near Crandall, Wyoming, shot her in the state's first wolf season. She was six.

Yellowstone's wolves have been studied down to the color of their fur. For almost twenty years, close to half of them were black, and most of the rest gray.

A white wolf on brown winter grass is not hard to see.

Day forty-one. A ridge above a hay field, an hour after sunrise. You're watching the elk. Somebody else is watching you.

Khaleesi. Forty-one days. Beaten by a line on a map.

Twenty-three days. Forty-one days. One wolf left. To win, he has to do something no dire wolf in the fossil record ever did.

Run three.

You're Remus. When you were eight months old, Colossal said you were stepping into the alpha role, in their words, "with quiet observation and strategic thinking." You watched your brother fight. You watched your sister leave.

So you do neither. You watch.

The packs out here are not as strong as they look. In 2025, Yellowstone's wolves had their worst year for pups since 1996. At least 36 were born. Fewer than half lived to the end of the year. The likely cause was canine distemper, a virus that kills pups far more easily than adults.

So the packs have gaps where the young wolves should be.

You keep your distance. You eat what the packs leave. A carcass after the ravens, a bison calf the winter already killed. There are nearly 5,300 bison in this park. Wolves rarely try a healthy adult bison. The winter does that work for them.

Day sixty, you get it wrong. You come over a rise straight into a pack on a kill, close enough to hear them eat. Romulus stood his ground at the creek bed. You do the opposite. Tail down, head low, you back away one step at a time, and you don't stop until the snow is between you. They let you go. They have meat, and you're one wolf.

Day seventy. Day eighty. You pass Khaleesi's forty-one without a single fight.

And then one pack stops chasing you off.

This next part is our guess, so here's what it rests on. Young wolves leave the pack they were born in all the time, and some of them get taken in by another pack. Yellowstone's biologists have watched lone males join packs before. A family that just lost most of its pups has more reason than most to let a new adult in.

To that pack, you don't smell like an ice age animal. You smell like a wolf.

Because you are one.

Remember Perri's study. The real dire wolf left no trace of ever mixing with gray wolves. If the real dire wolf had walked into this valley, there would have been no family to join. Just a fight, every winter, until it lost.

You were built from a gray wolf. Twenty changes on 14 genes.

And this park has taken in a bit of dog before. In 2009, a study in the journal Science traced the black coat of Yellowstone's black wolves to a gene that came from domestic dogs, picked up when wolves and dogs mated long ago. For years, nearly half the wolves out here carried a piece of dog. You only had one for a mother, and she gave you none of her genes.

Day ninety. Day one hundred. In March, the grizzlies wake up and come out to the same carcasses. You stay behind the bigger wolves and let them argue with the bears.

Romulus, twenty-three days. Khaleesi, forty-one. By April, you are the only one of the three still running.

In our version, somewhere in the north of the park, there's a pale male in a pack of gray wolves. Colossal still calls him a dire wolf, and the experts who advise the IUCN say he never was. The pack doesn't care.

The only dire wolf who makes it through a Yellowstone winter is the one who stops being a dire wolf.
