You've seen the photo: a small mouse with thick golden fur, shared widely and
described as a mammoth breakthrough. So why does bringing back a giant start with
the smallest animal in the room?

Is it cheaper to work with mice? Is the photo just there for headlines? Are
scientists stalling before the real work begins? Or is a mouse simply the nearest
thing they can get to a mammoth right now?

The myth needs clearing up first. Researchers combined genetic variants that already
exist naturally in ordinary, healthy mice. Each variant on its own was already known
to be safe, present in mouse populations long before this project began. They simply
brought several of them together in one mouse for the first time.

The tool behind all of this is CRISPR, a gene-editing method that works a bit like
molecular scissors. Scientists first found it inside bacteria, where it works as a
natural defense against viruses. Researchers have used it to edit animal genes since
the early 2010s. Two of the scientists behind it, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle
Charpentier, later won the 2020 Nobel Prize for the discovery. It cuts DNA at one
exact spot. Scientists can then swap that piece for a different, already-existing
version of the same gene. The version comes from another animal that already
carries it safely. Doctors have also started using the same tool to treat certain
human genetic diseases.

This mouse, presented by Colossal Biosciences in March 2025, carries seven edited
genes. Two of the mice from this project were even given names, Chip and Dale. The
names were borrowed from the classic Disney chipmunk duo. To find those seven
genes, researchers first studied preserved mammoth DNA, looking for the stretches
linked to cold-weather survival. Then they searched a mouse's own genome for
matching genes already sitting there. The edits gave it a thicker, wavier coat with
a golden tint. They also brought a slower metabolism and traits linked to handling
cold. Those are the same pressures a mammoth's fur once answered. None of the seven
genes is unique to mammoths. The company called this mouse a proof of concept. It
was a small, working demonstration that the method holds together, run before
anyone risks it on an actual mammoth. Mammoth-scale experiments are slow and hugely
expensive. Confirming the idea works on a small, disposable animal first saves
years of wasted effort later. According to Colossal's own researchers, more
iterations of the woolly mouse are already planned. Every future version will test
another small piece of the mammoth puzzle.

Why run that test on a mouse instead of an elephant? The reason comes down to the
calendar. A mouse is pregnant for about twenty days. An Asian elephant, the
mammoth's closest living relative, carries a pregnancy for around twenty-two
months, almost two full school years. Asian elephants are also an endangered
species, so researchers can't afford to risk many of them on untested edits. They
also give birth to one calf at a time, so there's no way to run several tests at
once. Zoos that care for Asian elephants often wait years between successful
pregnancies. Edit a gene into an elephant embryo, and a mistake might not surface
for two full years. Nearly two years of care and monitoring ride on a single
embryo. Edit the same idea into a mouse, and you'll know within three weeks, long
before anyone gets attached to the outcome.

Picture a bakery about to bake a three-tier wedding cake. It bakes one small test
cupcake first, using that exact recipe, the same flour and oven temperature. If the
batter turns out wrong, the bakery has only lost a cupcake and an afternoon.
Professional bakeries often test a new recipe this way before committing a full
sheet of expensive ingredients to it. Once the cupcake comes out right, scaling it
up to three tiers is just a matter of time and careful measuring. The recipe scales
up unchanged from cupcake to cake. A mouse plays that same role inside a genetics
lab. It's cheap to lose and quick to check, so it tells you the truth fast.

Housing plays a role too. A cage of mice fits on a single shelf and costs very
little to maintain. A single elephant needs acres of land and years of specialized
veterinary care. Multiply that cost by the number of test rounds a gene-editing
project usually needs.

But mice aren't the only small stand-in scientists use for this kind of work, and
not every project even reaches for a real one. Take the thylacine, the striped,
dog-like marsupial that once hunted across Australia and Tasmania. The last known
one died in a Tasmanian zoo in 1936. Zookeepers had nicknamed that last individual
Benjamin. Decades of hunting and habitat loss had already pushed the species to the
edge. The Tasmanian government had even paid bounties for every one killed.
Colossal is working to bring it back too, alongside the University of Melbourne's
TIGRR Lab. It's a research group built specifically around thylacine genetics. You
might expect the same trick here. Edit some mouse genes, test the method, then
scale up to a thylacine embryo. Here the pattern breaks. The team turned to the
fat-tailed dunnart, a tiny carnivorous marsupial often nicknamed the marsupial
mouse. The dunnart looks and moves like a mouse. It's actually a distant relative
of the thylacine itself, born tiny and raised mostly inside its mother's pouch.
Andrew Pask, a researcher on the project, gave the exact reason for choosing it
over an ordinary mouse. The dunnart is pregnant for just thirteen and a half days,
even faster than a mouse's twenty. A faster, closer relative sat right there, ready
to use. Finishing first is what earns an animal the job, no matter how small it is
or what family it belongs to.

The thylacine's problem was different from the dire wolf's. Its closest living
relatives are other marsupials. Those marsupials are far more distantly related to
it than a gray wolf is to a dire wolf.

Mice have played this role for more than a century, ever since hobbyists breeding
fancy mice earned early geneticists their first reliable lab animal. They breed
fast and cost little to house, and by the early 2000s their entire genome was
mapped in detail. That head start is why almost any new gene-editing method still
gets tried on a mouse before it moves to something bigger and slower.

But "every" doesn't hold up literally. Colossal's dire wolf project, an Ice Age
predator gone for more than ten thousand years, skipped the small stand-in
completely. Dire wolves once ranged across much of North America before vanishing
at the end of that Ice Age. Researchers pulled genetic material from ancient dire
wolf fossils. They compared it against the genome of the living gray wolf, the dire
wolf's closest modern relative. Then they made their edits directly on that gray
wolf material, with no mouse phase and no dunnart phase in between. The edited
embryos went straight into a large mixed-breed dog acting as the surrogate mother.
Three healthy pups, Romulus, Remus, and Khaleesi, were born from that surrogate.
Why skip the small stand-in this time? The gray wolf is already such a close
relative of the dire wolf. Far fewer genetic changes were needed to make the shift.
Fewer edits meant less uncertainty riding on each one. The extra testing step
simply wasn't worth the time it would add. The honest answer to the question in the
title is a real, qualified yes and no. Most of these projects lean on a small proxy
first. The target species is usually too slow or too distant to risk testing on
directly. At least one skipped that step entirely, because its target was already
close enough to begin with.

This pattern shows up outside mammals too. Colossal's artificial egg project
follows the same logic, using a 3D-printed lattice shell built to mimic a real
eggshell. The shell's structure is built from thousands of tiny hexagons, chosen
because that shape lets oxygen pass through efficiently. It took about eighteen
days inside that device to reach the same stage a normal egg reaches by day
twenty-one. Twenty-six ordinary chickens were hatched first inside that device,
before anyone tries it on a moa egg, an extinct bird from New Zealand. Some moa
stood up to three and a half meters tall, more than eleven feet. A single moa egg
held roughly eighty times the volume of a chicken egg. Its own eggs are too rare to
risk on an untested method. The Associated Press covered the hatchings in May 2026.
It called the technology impressive while noting that true de-extinction remains
unproven. Wings or fur, feathers or coats, the underlying test looks the same.

The small animal gets picked for one simple reason. Whichever species can finish a
full pregnancy and hand back an answer the fastest gets the job, every single time.
The same logic holds whether the target is a mammoth or a bird species nobody has
seen alive in centuries.

So picture that first photo again, the small golden mouse sitting next to headlines
about mammoths. It was just the fastest animal willing to answer the question
first. Nobody in a lab coat looked twice at an elephant until much later.

The same rule shows up everywhere in biology, all the way down to how long a tooth
needs to last. If you want to see that second rule in action, there's a video on
this channel called "Why Don't Wild Animals Need Dentists?" It looks at wild teeth
that never see a dentist and somehow hold up anyway. An animal's body gets built
for the demands of its actual life. A lion's teeth are one example. They last
exactly as long as a lion needs them, no longer. The next time something in nature
looks like a choice or a kindness, ask what deadline it was built around. That's
usually where the real answer is hiding.
