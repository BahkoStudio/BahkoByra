## Del 2: Manuset

By April 2026, scientists had made 39 northern white rhino embryos and frozen them in Berlin and Italy. The only two northern white rhinos alive can't carry any of them. They are Najin and her daughter Fatu. Both are female, and with no male left, the conservancy that guards them calls the northern white functionally extinct: alive, but unable to breed on its own. And in June, the scientists running the rescue gave their own paper a title you'd expect for an animal that's already gone. Here are fifteen facts about the last two northern white rhinos that make no sense. We end with a rhino that died years ago and may still become a mother.

Fact one. The first rhino pregnancy from a lab-made embryo was only discovered at an autopsy, the exam after death. In September 2023, at Ol Pejeta Conservancy in Kenya, the team placed one such embryo into a surrogate, a female who carries another animal's calf. Her name was Curra. Both the embryo and Curra were southern white rhinos, the common kind. In late November 2023, Curra was found dead. Extreme rain had brought bacterial spores up from deep in the soil, and she died of a severe Clostridia infection. The autopsy showed she was 70 days pregnant, with a male 6.4 centimetres long. DNA confirmed he came from the transferred embryo. "We are overwhelmed by the fact that this technique works so well," said Thomas Hildebrandt of the Leibniz Institute for Zoo and Wildlife Research in Berlin, who leads the rescue project, called BioRescue. The method had worked. The rain had won.

Fact two. Some of the frozen embryos pair Fatu with her own half-brother. It starts at a zoo in Dvůr Králové, in what is now the Czech Republic. There, in 1980, a male called Saut fathered a son, Suni. Nineteen years later, Saut fathered a second calf, with Najin. That calf was Fatu, born in 2000. So Suni and Fatu share a father. And Suni's mother, Nasima, was Najin's mother too, by a male called Sudan, who we'll meet again. So Suni is also Fatu's mother's half-brother. His frozen sperm is one of only two males' sperm used to make the embryos.

Fact three. To get the eggs, scientists go in through the rectum. The device is nearly two metres long and guided by ultrasound. A needle passes through the wall of the gut to reach the ovary and draws an egg out. Nobody had ever collected eggs from a northern white, so the team built the technique and equipment "entirely from scratch." The first collection took place at Ol Pejeta on 22 August 2019, with both females under general anaesthetic, and it produced ten eggs. They were flown to the Avantea lab in Cremona, Italy, and fertilised with frozen sperm. By September 2019, the first two pure northern white embryos existed. According to AFP, Fatu has now been fully sedated for this more than twenty times.

Fact four. Taking eggs from Fatu again and again seems to have made her ovaries healthier. Hildebrandt's team had already reviewed 65 sets of these procedures on white rhinos. Their 2023 paper judged them safe, and found that regular egg collection helped the ovaries of older females that had never had a calf. Then in August 2025, BioRescue and Safari Park Dvůr Králové reported that Fatu's ovarian health "actually appears to have benefited from repeated procedures." They call it Ovarian Cleansing Therapy.

Fact five. The two females can't carry a calf for two completely different reasons. In December 2014, vets examined both under anaesthesia. Najin's reproductive organs were healthy, but she has weak hind legs. According to Safari Park Dvůr Králové, those legs make it hard for her to hold up a male during mating, and could cause problems in pregnancy. Fatu has degenerative lesions, damaged patches of tissue, in her uterus, where a calf would grow. Then Najin's eggs failed too. Over three collections she gave ten eggs in all, and none of them could be fertilised. In October 2021, at 32, she was retired as an egg donor after an ethics review. Ultrasound had found "multiple small, benign tumours", harmless growths, in her cervix and uterus, and a cyst, a fluid-filled sac, 25 centimetres across in her left ovary. Every embryo made so far comes from Fatu.

Fact six. One small Czech zoo is the only zoo where northern white rhinos ever bred. In its own words, Dvůr Králové was "the only animal park where northern white rhinos had bred." Suni, born there in 1980, was the first northern white ever born in captivity. San Diego held eight wild-caught northern whites over the decades and never bred one. Fatu, nicknamed the "Baby of the Millennium," was the last northern white born at the Czech zoo. In October 2014, Suni was found dead in his enclosure at Ol Pejeta, and no cause was ever established. Two months later, Angalifu, the other male whose sperm the scientists use, died in San Diego. That left Sudan as the last northern white male.

Fact seven. The future last male flew home to Africa on a jumbo jet and was let out of his crate for carrots. Sudan was born wild in 1973, in what is now South Sudan. In 1975, as a calf, he was caught by the team of Josef Vágner, director of the Dvůr Králové zoo. The biologist Kes Hillman Smith wrote that he "became habituated to humans quicker than most," meaning he got used to people fast, and "was a favourite in the capture camp." At the Czech zoo, she wrote, Sudan and the other young rhinos caught with him "learned to adapt to the different climate of seasonal snows." In December 2009, Sudan flew back to Africa with Suni, Najin and Fatu, three rhinos who had never seen Africa. The hope was that natural surroundings would get them breeding. They went by specially chartered Boeing 747 to Nairobi, then by road to Ol Pejeta. Mark Carwardine, who was on that flight, later wrote: "The male, Sudan, was so relaxed during the flight that we let him out of his crate and fed him carrots while we ate our sandwiches."

Fact eight. The last male northern white rhino had a Tinder profile. In April 2017, Ol Pejeta and Tinder put Sudan on the dating app as "The Most Eligible Bachelor in the World." The aim was to raise nine million dollars for research into breeding with help from a lab. Swiping right, the app's way of saying yes, took you to a donation page. His profile said: "I don't mean to be too forward, but the fate of the species literally depends on me." It also said: "I perform well under pressure." According to the Associated Press, it was shown in 190 countries and over 40 languages, and the conservancy's website crashed. Sudan's 2014 health check had found his sperm were few, slow and partly abnormal. On 19 March 2018, aged about 45 and no longer able to stand, he was put to sleep.

Fact nine. One of the first people to write the name down was disappointed by the colour. In a letter from 1802, the oldest Dutch record of the white rhino, Petrus Borcherds wrote this. "I expected this animal to be entirely white according to its name, but found that she was a paler ash-grey than the black." You may have heard that "white" is a mishearing of wyd, a Dutch or Afrikaans word meaning wide, for the rhino's broad mouth. It's a myth. In a 2007 article in Pachyderm, the rhino journal, John Feely found no historical record of anyone using wyd for a rhino. That story began as a guess in 1931. Most of a rhino's colour comes from the mud it rolls in. The charity Save the Rhino says there is "no colour difference" between black and white rhinos.

Fact ten. Fatu's horn is trimmed so she won't hurt her mother. In 2025, AFP reported that Fatu had become "a little bit grumpy" and sometimes tries to fight Najin, "so rangers trim her horn to prevent injuries." Fatu spends almost all her time with a wild southern white female, brought in to show her life outside a zoo. Najin, the mother, spends much of her time alone. She is calm, the caregivers' favourite, and prone to what AFP called "bouts of flatulence." Najin and Fatu live under armed guards around the clock, with a watchtower and sniffer dogs.

Fact eleven. Scientists can't agree on whether the northern white is its own species. Up close, according to AFP, northern whites are smaller than their southern cousins, "with fluffier ears and slightly longer tails." The rescue team's own paper treats them as a subspecies, a local branch of one species. In 2010, the researchers Groves, Fernando and Robovský argued for a full species, split from the southern white for "over a million years." In 2018, a study in the journal Genome Research read the DNA of northern whites from frozen cells. It found the two kinds differ by about 0.1 percent, and put the split at less than 80,000 years ago. One estimate is more than ten times the other.

Fact twelve. Down to fifteen animals, the last wild herd doubled. The last wild northern whites lived in Garamba, in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo. In 1960 there were over 1,000. By 1984, when a protection project began, only 15 were left. Kes Hillman Smith, a biologist there for 25 years, wrote that with protection, "numbers of both rhinos and elephants doubled in eight years." During the war in Congo in 1997, all rangers were disarmed for three months, yet five calves were born. When armed horsemen began crossing from Sudan in 2004, a plan to move a few rhinos to Ol Pejeta "caused a political storm and had to be shelved." The last confirmed sighting came from the air in March 2006. A survey in 2008 found none.

Fact thirteen. Rhinos are being flown into Garamba again, and they are the other kind. In July 2023, sixteen southern white rhinos were flown from South Africa into the park where the northern whites died out. In December 2025, 24 more arrived. Peter Fearnhead, chief executive of the conservation group African Parks, said the southern white, "as the closest genetic alternative, can fulfil the role of the Northern white rhino." A century ago, southern whites were nearly gone themselves. In the early 1900s they fell to fewer than a hundred; by some counts, as few as twenty survived, in Umfolozi, South Africa. By the end of 2024 there were 15,752 white rhinos, according to the conservation groups IUCN and TRAFFIC.

Fact fourteen. No northern white pregnancy has held yet, and the first calf is already promised to the Czech zoo. Since Curra, there have been six transfers of pure northern white embryos into southern white surrogates: July 2024, December 2024, May 2025, and three more in the second half of 2025. None turned into a lasting pregnancy. The team points out that human IVF, making a baby from an egg fertilised in a lab, took more than 100 attempts before it worked. In January 2024, Hildebrandt said he hoped for a northern white birth "in two to three years," which runs out in early 2027. Najin is 37 now, and in 2025 AFP reported she was expected to live another five to ten years. If a calf comes, BioRescue has already decided who gets it: "The Safari Park Dvůr Králové receives every first calf, and Kenya Wildlife Service receives every second calf."

Fact fifteen. A rhino dead for more than ten years may still become a mother. Nabire, Najin's sister, died at Dvůr Králové on 27 July 2015, aged about 31, when a huge cyst burst. Scientists turned skin cells from Nabire into stem cells, cells that can become almost any other kind of cell. In 2022, they used those to make early versions of the cells that become eggs and sperm, a first for a large mammal. Jan Stejskal of Safari Park Dvůr Králové said "it seems perfectly possible that a direct descendant of Nabire will be born at some point in the future." The same route could one day give Najin the embryos her own eggs never did. Turning those early cells into real eggs is still unsolved.

In June 2026, the team published their work under the title "De-extinction of the Northern white rhinoceros." De-extinction is the word people use for mammoths and dodos. Two northern whites are still alive in Kenya, and the rest of the 39 embryos made by April are still waiting in freezers in Berlin and Italy.
