## Del 2: Manuset

In September 2026, the geologist Brigid Lynch pulled an oddly shaped rock out of a creek in a preserve south of San Francisco. A reverse image search told her it was a tooth. Palaeontologists told her whose: a mastodon's. It was over eight inches long. Finds like it are routine. Michigan alone has over 250 mastodon sites, and the mastodon is its official state fossil. Nobody has a plan to bring it back. Fifteen facts about the mastodon that make no sense, ending with a mastodon in San Diego that some scientists say humans butchered 130,000 years ago.

Fact one. The first fossil skeleton ever mounted in America was built by a portrait painter, and the tusks went on upside down. Nobody alive had seen the animal, so in 1801 Charles Willson Peale and his team pointed the tusks at the floor, like a walrus, and billed it as a flesh-eater. Peale had bought the bones from a marl pit, a farmer's clay dig, near Newburgh, New York, for three hundred dollars. By Christmas 1801 the skeleton stood in Philadelphia, and that winter thirteen men ate dinner at a round table inside the ribcage. The tusks were turned the right way up in 1826, and in the 1840s the skeleton was sold to a museum in Darmstadt, Germany, where it still stands.

Fact two. The first mastodon tooth on record was bought for a quarter-pint of rum and sent to London as proof of giants. On 30 July 1705, the Boston News-Letter reported a giant's teeth dug up at Claverack, New York. Lord Cornbury, the governor of New York, wrote to the Royal Society that a local politician had bought one from a Dutch countryman for a gill of rum, a quarter of a pint. The Boston preacher Cotton Mather took it as proof of the giants of Genesis. Eighty years later, a Virginia lawyer wrote that the animal might still be walking around somewhere.

Fact three. Thomas Jefferson believed the mastodon might still be alive out West. In his 1785 book Notes on the State of Virginia, he called it a mammoth and suggested it might survive in the unexplored north and west of the continent. The French naturalist Buffon had claimed New World animals were smaller and weaker. The longest chapter of Jefferson's only book answers Buffon with numbers. The mastodon, which he called "the largest of all terrestrial beings", meaning land animals, was his strongest card. Monticello's historians say Jefferson told Lewis and Clark to look for mastodons. In 1807 he paid for William Clark to dig at Big Bone Lick, Kentucky, and some 300 bones and teeth arrived in three boxes. Jefferson laid them out in the President's House, today's White House. Duplicates went to Paris.

Fact four. Paris had mastodon bones for sixty years before anyone knew what they were, and filed them as an elephant's leg with a hippo's teeth. The bones came from Big Bone Lick, Kentucky, in 1739, collected by the Native guides of a French expedition on the Ohio. In 1762 the naturalist Daubenton decided the thigh bone and tusk were an elephant's, but the molars, the big back teeth, unlike any elephant tooth, must belong to a giant hippopotamus. In 1768 William Hunter told the Royal Society the Ohio animal differed from living elephants and must therefore be extinct. Georges Cuvier, in 1796, compared living elephants with the Siberian mammoth and the "animal of Ohio", and argued the fossil ones no longer existed. Ten years later Cuvier named it "mastodonte", from the Greek for nipple and tooth, after the rounded bumps on its molars. He also asked Americans to stop calling it a mammoth. He was seven years too late.

Fact five. The mastodon's scientific name means mammoth, and the name Mastodon is officially wrong. In 1799 Johann Friedrich Blumenbach gave the bones their own genus, the group name, and chose Mammut, the German word for mammoth, because he assumed they were mammoths. Cuvier's genus Mastodon came in 1817. In zoology the oldest name wins, so Mammut is valid and Mastodon is a junior synonym, a second name for the same animal that lost on a technicality. The American Museum's Henry Fairfield Osborn called Mammut a "barbaric term" in 1936 and proposed ignoring the rule. He lost. In St Louis in 1840, a showman found a different way to be wrong about the animal.

Fact six. A 32-foot fake mastodon went on show in London, and its bones are still on display there. Albert Koch dug mastodon bones in Missouri and in 1840 mounted a skeleton in his St Louis museum as the Missouri Leviathan. It stood fifteen feet tall and thirty-two feet long, with extra vertebrae spliced into the spine and the tusks pointing out sideways. In February 1842 the London anatomist Richard Owen told the Geological Society it was a composite of several mastodons with added bones. The British Museum bought it anyway. Owen had it taken apart and realigned the tusks, and the result, an ordinary American mastodon, is what visitors see today in the Natural History Museum in London. More than half of the mastodon skeletons one study checked carry a mark of their own.

Fact seven. Fifty-nine of 113 mastodon skeletons carry the scar of tuberculosis. In 2006 Bruce Rothschild and Richard Laub examined 113 mastodon skeletons with foot bones. Fifty-nine of them, 52 per cent, carried a bone scar that in bison is a sure sign of tuberculosis. In humans, only about one to seven per cent of infected people ever develop bone damage. So, Rothschild and Laub argued, the infection must have been almost everywhere. They called it "pandemic, a hyperdisease", a disease that can take out a whole species, and "one of several probable factors contributing to mastodon extinction". Rothschild read disease off the feet. Daniel Fisher read something else off the tusks.

Fact eight. A bull mastodon's tusk recorded a fight almost every spring after it turned twenty. In 1999 a family deepening a pond in Hyde Park, New York, turned up an almost complete male, carbon-dated to about 11,480 years. Fisher, of the University of Michigan, found rows of shallow pits along the outer curve of its tusks. The pits sat in ivory formed between mid-spring and early summer, in nearly every year after the animal turned twenty. He linked it to musth, the yearly period when bull elephants turn aggressive. A bull swung its tusk tip up into an opponent, and the impact jammed the tusk back into its socket, crushing the cells that make ivory. The Hyde Park bones went to a museum. In Manhattan, a mastodon jaw went to a milkman.

Fact nine. A milkman stole most of a mastodon's jaw in Manhattan, and the palaeontologist who came for the rest was pickpocketed. In 1925 a construction crew on Seamen Avenue in Inwood, at the top of Manhattan, hit the lower jaw and teeth of a young mastodon. A milkman ran off with most of it. By the time C. C. Mook arrived from the American Museum of Natural History, three teeth were left, and two of those were lifted from his pocket on the way to his car. According to Carl Mehling of the same museum, over a dozen mastodon fossils have been found within New York City, and almost none still exist. In Ohio in 1989, one came out of the ground almost whole, and left the country anyway.

Fact ten. An 11,000-year-old mastodon came out of a golf course with living bacteria in its gut, and was sold to Japan. In December 1989 a man running a digging crane at the Burning Tree Golf Course in Heath, Ohio, hit a skull. Wood found with the skeleton carbon-dated to between 11,450 and 11,660 years. Its last meal was still inside: moss, seeds, leaves, water lilies and swamp grass. In 1998 microbiologists reported dozens of species of still-living bacteria from the gut contents. The skeleton itself had already gone: in 1993 it was reportedly sold for more than 600,000 dollars, and it is now in a museum in Japan. Far to the north, the dates on museum bones were wrong by tens of thousands of years.

Fact eleven. Mastodons lived in the Arctic, but only on vacation, and museum varnish hid that for decades. In 2014 Grant Zazula and colleagues redated 36 mastodon fossils from Alaska and the Yukon, measuring only the bone's own protein. The old dates had included varnish and glue painted onto the specimens, which made the bones look far younger. Most were older than radiocarbon can measure, beyond about 50,000 years. Mastodons probably moved into the Arctic only briefly, around 125,000 years ago, when it was as warm as today. They were gone by about 75,000 years ago, long before the first humans crossed from Asia. Zazula said: "The residency of mastodons in the north did not last long." Zazula dated the bones. A team in Ontario went further and read the DNA.

Fact twelve. One mastodon may be half a million years old, and Mexico may hide a third species. In September 2025 Emil Karpinski and colleagues at McMaster University reported an American mastodon, the eastern of the two known species, that may be about 500,000 years old. Mexican specimens, McMaster said, could be an older branch of its western cousin, or "possibly an entirely new, third mastodon species". The same team had already read a small loop of DNA, the kind passed down only from mother to child, in more than thirty mastodons, and sorted the animals into groups. The groups arose at different times, which they read as mastodons pushing north in every warm spell and dying out there in every cold one. That western species sat unnamed in museum drawers for decades.

Fact thirteen. The Pacific mastodon was only named in 2019. On 27 March 2019, Alton Dooley Jr and colleagues described it from fossils in California and southern Idaho. It had narrower teeth, six hip vertebrae where the American mastodon usually has five, and no lower tusks at any age. The American mastodon, mostly its young males, sometimes kept a pair of small tusks in the lower jaw. Dooley's team argued every ice age mastodon ever found in California belongs to this species, so the famous American mastodon never lived in California at all. If Dooley's team is right, Brigid Lynch's creek tooth is a Pacific mastodon's, an intact adult molar 8.5 inches long. Wayne Thompson of Pacific Paleontology called it "the most spectacular mastodon molar that I've ever seen". The preserve's headline said 10,000 years old; Thompson has put it at 50,000 to 100,000. The man who read the Hyde Park tusks also worked out what hunters did with the meat.

Fact fourteen. Ice Age hunters stored mastodon meat at the bottom of ponds, and a dead draft horse proved it works. In the late 1980s Daniel Fisher was excavating an 11,000-year-old butchered mastodon at the Heisler site in southern Michigan. With it were anchors, apparently made from mastodon intestine filled with mud, to hold the meat down, away from scavengers. From 1989 Fisher sank lamb and deer meat in a Michigan pond, and it was still "reasonably fresh" inside the following spring. In the 1990s he and Catherine Badgley butchered a 1,500-pound draft horse and sank it in a winter pond. Months later, Badgley said, the meat still looked like "fresh red meat from the supermarket". Fisher's mastodon met people about 11,000 years ago. The last one on this list may have met them far earlier, if its excavators are right.

Fact fifteen. A mastodon in San Diego is the evidence for the claim that humans were in America 130,000 years ago. In 1992, as a state highway was being widened, Richard Cerutti of the San Diego Natural History Museum found mastodon bones, and a team under Thomas Deméré excavated them. A method that measures how much of the bone's uranium has turned into thorium gave about 130,700 years, give or take 9,400. In 2017, in the journal Nature, Steven Holen and colleagues argued that rounded stones lying with the broken bones were hammers and anvils. The twisting breaks, the kind only fresh bone makes, showed the bones had been smashed for marrow, they said. If so, people were in the Americas more than 110,000 years before the oldest widely accepted sites. Most archaeologists who have answered say no. The archaeologist Michael Waters said there are no clear stone tools and the site "is likely just an interesting paleontological locality". Deméré answered that his critics relied on "long-distance interpretations of our data".

In August 2026 Colossal, the company trying to revive the mammoth, told TIME its first mammoth is now expected in the "early 2030s", and the article never mentions the mastodon. Brigid Lynch's tooth is at Stanford now, where curator Christine Garcia's team will carbon-date it and 3D-print a copy for the preserve. Radiocarbon runs out at about 50,000 years. If Thompson is right about the age, the machine may come back with no answer at all. Three hundred and twenty-one years after a Dutch countryman sold one of these teeth for a quarter-pint of rum, the newest one is sitting in a lab, waiting for a date.
