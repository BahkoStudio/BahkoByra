## Del 2: Manuset

In August 2026, Colossal Biosciences posted a 23-second video listing the animals it probably won't bring back. There were only two.

The first was the giant short-faced bear, an extinct Ice Age bear that Colossal said would be an "instant super apex predator," a top hunter with nothing above it. The second was megalodon, the giant shark. The person in the video said they get a lot of requests for megalodon, and didn't understand why.

The only reason given for either was danger. But megalodon was never possible anyway. It died out about 3.6 million years ago. And the IUCN, the world's main conservation body, says in its guidelines on de-extinction that the technology can reach back "hundreds of thousands of years," but "not to millions of years."

That leaves the six animals Colossal is working on: the woolly mammoth, the Tasmanian tiger, the dodo, the dire wolf, the moa, and the bluebuck. So which of the six is actually worth it, and in what order?

Scientists wrote down a way to answer that. When we put Colossal's six through it, the mammoth lands in the bottom half. And the best result in the scientists' own paper went to an animal Colossal isn't working on at all.

In 2014, three conservation scientists, Philip Seddon, Axel Moehrenschlager and John Ewen, published a paper in the journal Trends in Ecology and Evolution. Bringing an animal back, they argued, is only step one. Then you have to put it somewhere. So they wrote ten yes-or-no questions, built on the thinking used for moving living animals into the wild. In their paper, an animal that fails any one of them is ruled out.

A few of the ten: Can the causes of the extinction be fixed? Is there enough habitat, now and in the future? Do local people accept it? Is the risk to people acceptable? And if it all goes wrong, can the animals be removed? Danger to people, the only reason in Colossal's video, is just one of the ten.

The paper also gave the usual de-extinction wish lists a nickname, borrowed from the writer Alex Steffen: "charismatic necrofauna." Roughly, dead animals people find appealing. Two years later, the IUCN built these ideas into its guidelines. It said the candidates so far had been species that are "beloved, missed, charismatic." In a 2021 Ipsos poll of Americans, 22 percent picked the dodo and 19 the woolly mammoth, and 30 percent wanted no species back at all.

The IUCN also set the rule that matters most here. The only good reason to bring an animal back, it says, is to restore a job it once did in nature. Without that, de-extinction "would seem unjustified." And if a living species could do the same job better, with less cost or risk, you should use the living one. The guidelines add that the risk grows the longer an animal has been gone, and the more the copy differs from the original.

The 2014 paper only screened one of Colossal's six. So for this ranking, we're applying these published rules ourselves, using only what's on the record. It takes two steps. First, is there a specific job in nature, on the record, that the animal would restore? Animals with no job named, or with a living animal already doing it, go to the bottom. Second, for the rest, how long has it been gone, and how close a copy can actually be made?

Worst to best.

Number six: the dire wolf.

In April 2025, Colossal announced three animals it called dire wolves: Romulus, Remus and Khaleesi. They were made by editing 20 places in 14 genes of the gray wolf, then cloning the result.

Eleven days later, the IUCN's own wolf experts, the Canid Specialist Group, gave their verdict: "The three animals produced by Colossal are not dire wolves." The dire wolf hunted mammoths and giant ground sloths, and those are gone. And the gray wolf already fills its place.

Measured against the IUCN's rules, that's two strikes: no lost job to restore, and a living animal already doing it. Colossal's chief scientist, Beth Shapiro, later told New Scientist: "Our animals are grey wolves with 20 edits that are cloned." The real dire wolf has been gone for more than 10,000 years. It's the only one of the six Colossal says it has already made, and by these rules it's the hardest to justify.

Number five: the dodo.

The dodo fails the first step for a simpler reason. Colossal's own dodo page, checked in October 2026, doesn't name a job in nature that the dodo would restore. In the launch coverage in 2023, MIT Technology Review reported Shapiro calling it "the poster child, in a sad way, for how human habitat alteration can drive species to extinction." That's a symbol, and the IUCN's core rule asks for a job.

It's also a bird, and birds are a special problem. The IUCN said in 2016 that cloning a bird "currently seems impossible." Colossal is taking a different route, growing germ cells, the cells that become eggs and sperm, from pigeons.

One strike, where the dire wolf had two. That puts the dodo fifth.

Number four: the woolly mammoth.

The mammoth comes with the biggest promise on the list. Colossal's case is that herds of big grazers would trample the snow and keep the Arctic's frozen ground colder. That frozen ground, called permafrost, holds 1,600 billion metric tons of carbon, the company says. So the mammoth clears the first step, on Colossal's word.

The second step is where it struggles. Mammoths have been gone for about 4,000 years. And according to the IUCN, it's "highly unlikely" that any mammoth cells good enough for cloning still exist.

Even with good cells, cloning is brutal. Only one extinct animal has ever been cloned: a wild goat from the Pyrenees, the bucardo. Scientists froze skin cells from the last one, a female named Celia, before she died in 2000. They built 439 cloned embryos and transferred them into 57 surrogate mothers. One baby goat was born, in 2003. It died within minutes, from a defect in its lungs.

So instead of cloning a mammoth, Colossal is editing Asian elephant cells toward one, with a target of around 85 gene changes. An elephant pregnancy lasts about 22 months. Colossal once promised the first calf for 2028. Colossal's CEO, Ben Lamm, has since told TIME: "We are thinking it will be in the early 2030s." And changing the Arctic would take whole herds.

Back in 2015, before Colossal existed, the mammoth expert Tori Herridge wrote in a book review that mammoths are being worked on "because mammoths are magnificent." Of the animals that clear the first step, the mammoth has been gone the longest, and probably has no cells left to clone. That puts it fourth.

Number three: the moa.

The moa were giant flightless birds from New Zealand. There were nine species, and the South Island giant moa stood about 3.6 meters tall. All of them were gone around 600 years ago, mainly from hunting. Colossal announced the project in July 2025, backed by the film director Peter Jackson.

The moa has a job on the record. Colossal says moa were important seed dispersers, carrying seeds to new places.

Then there's one of the ten questions: do local people accept it? Here, the reaction splits. Kyle Davis of the Ngāi Tahu tribe told AP the project had "really reinvigorated the interest in examining our own traditions and mythology." Nic Rawlence of the University of Otago told the journal EMBO Reports: "In all the engagement our lab has done across the motu with our indigenous research partners, there is no support for de-extinction." Motu means the country.

And the moa needs the same unproven bird route as the dodo. Stuart Pimm, an ecologist at Duke University, told AP he thinks it's "exceedingly unlikely that they could do this in any meaningful way." Still, it's been gone about 600 years, against the mammoth's 4,000. That puts it third.

Number two: the bluebuck.

It's the newest animal on Colossal's list, added in April 2026, and the least known. The bluebuck was an antelope from the grasslands near what is now Cape Town. It died out around 1800, only about 34 years after scientists first described it.

Its job is on the record, and it's specific. Colossal describes it as a grazer with specially evolved teeth that filled a narrow niche, and says bringing it back could benefit a whole group of native plant-eaters. That's the company's claim, but it names a job.

On the second step, it pulls ahead. It's been gone about 225 years. Colossal has its genome, its full set of DNA, read 40 times over from a specimen at the Swedish Museum of Natural History. A living relative, the roan antelope, gives it DNA to compare against. A 2017 paper in the journal Functional Ecology advises working on "species that went extinct recently rather than older extinctions." Gone barely two centuries, with its genome already read 40 times over. That puts it second.

Before number one, there's one thing the ten questions don't cover: money.

In 2017, a team led by Joseph Bennett published a study in Nature Ecology and Evolution. They compared the cost of keeping resurrected species alive in the wild with what the same money could do for living ones. In New South Wales, Australia, the money for five resurrected species could instead protect 42 living ones, more than eight times as many. Bennett told the Christian Science Monitor: "it makes sense to spend money on the living rather than the dead."

Colossal is funded by investors, with more than half a billion dollars raised, and it argues its tools help living animals too. There's real evidence the technology can help. In 2020, scientists cloned a black-footed ferret named Elizabeth Ann from cells frozen in 1988, carrying genes missing from a population that descends from just seven animals. She can't breed, but in 2024 another clone from the same cells, Antonia, had babies. And in late 2025, Colossal bought ViaGen, the company that did that cloning.

So the money question hangs over all six. On the published rules alone, one animal still comes out on top.

Number one: the Tasmanian tiger.

The last known thylacine died in 1936, in a zoo in Hobart. That makes it the most recent loss of the six.

It has a job to fill, by its own team's account. Andrew Pask of the University of Melbourne, a scientist on the project, has said it was Tasmania's only top predator, and that no other animal could fill its place.

It also has the most practical path. Pask points out that thylacines "give birth to babies that are not much bigger than a grain of rice." Compare that with a 22-month elephant pregnancy. In 2024, the team reported a thylacine genome estimated to be more than 99.9 percent accurate. It also reported more than 300 edits in the cells of its living stand-in, a small pouched animal called the fat-tailed dunnart.

Even Tori Herridge, who has criticized the mammoth project, told Der Spiegel in 2021 that she finds the Tasmanian tiger "exciting."

The most recent loss, a job on the record, and the smallest baby to grow. By these rules, it comes first.

It's also the only one of Colossal's six that the 2014 paper screened. And it still didn't get the best result.

In that paper, Seddon and his co-authors ran three animals through the ten questions: the Tasmanian tiger, the baiji, and a butterfly.

The Tasmanian tiger passed, with conditions. The authors flagged the risk to farmers' sheep, which they said "would have to be addressed by compensation packages," meaning farmers paid for lost animals.

The baiji, a river dolphin from China, failed the first two questions, about fixing the causes of its extinction. The paper reported that more than 15 billion cubic meters of wastewater flow into its river, the Yangtze, every year.

The third was the Xerces blue, a small blue butterfly from San Francisco, and it passed cleanly. It was last recorded in 1941, five years after the last Tasmanian tiger, and its final colony lived on a single patch of sand dune. Potential habitat for it still exists in Golden Gate Park.

So what makes an animal worth bringing back? By the published rules, it has a job nature is missing. It died out recently. It still has a home to go back to. And it can be removed if things go wrong.

The best result in the scientists' own test went to a butterfly whose last colony fit on a patch of dune 21 meters by 46 meters.

Colossal's list has the moa instead, a bird that stood up to 3.6 meters tall. And the moa only gets stranger. Watch "15 Facts About the De-Extinct Moa That Make No Sense" next.
