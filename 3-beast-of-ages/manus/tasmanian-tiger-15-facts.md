## Del 2: Manuset

In August 2026, a stuffed head with plastic googly eyes went up for auction in England. It was listed as a fox, with a guide price of about forty pounds. It sold for more than sixty thousand pounds. The buyer had counted its teeth. It was a thylacine, better known as the Tasmanian tiger. Here are fifteen facts about this animal that make no sense. We end with a dried specimen in Stockholm that gave scientists their first look at which genes were switched on inside an extinct animal.

Fact one. The previous owner had reportedly bought the head for fifty pounds at an antiques market, because the googly eyes made them laugh. The collector who bought it at auction, James Cranfield, looked at the teeth instead. A fox has six small upper front teeth between its fangs. This one had eight. "Everyone overlooked it as a tatty fox head," he said, "but I spotted the teeth." X-rays later showed a full, uncut skull under the stuffing. It is only the second known stuffed thylacine head made for a wall. The International Thylacine Specimen Database now records 815 thylacine specimens worldwide, and only sixteen of them are in private hands. One of those sixteen was bought as a joke.

Fact two. The males had a pouch too. In a paper published in 1891, the British zoologist Frank Beddard described a male from London Zoo. Its scrotum hung inside a partial pouch that opened backwards, like a bandicoot's. The best guess is that it protected the genitals from snagging in thick undergrowth. Among living marsupials, only one species has anything like it as an adult: the water opossum, or yapok, which tucks its testes into its pouch before it swims. The female's pouch opened backwards too. A mother could carry up to four young at a time, according to the Tasmanian government. As they grew, the pouch grew with them, until it reached almost to the ground.

Fact three is what happened inside that pouch. In 2018, a team led by Axel Newton CT-scanned every known pouch young catalogued as a thylacine, anywhere in the world. There were thirteen. At birth, a thylacine looked like any other newborn marsupial. By about twelve weeks, when it was ready to leave the pouch, it looked more like a puppy, with longer back legs than front legs. The dog shape arrived late. A newborn marsupial has to crawl into the pouch using its front legs, so those legs can't change much. That limit, the authors argue, is part of why the thylacine never got the full set of dog-like features. And two of the thirteen, held at the Tasmanian Museum and Art Gallery, turned out to be mislabelled. They were most likely baby Tasmanian devils or baby quolls, small spotted marsupial hunters.

Fact four. The adult skull looked so much like a dog's that professors at Oxford reportedly set it as a trick question in final exams. The zoologist Richard Dawkins tells the story of students learning that any dog-like skull on the table was probably the thylacine. Charles Darwin had already noticed the resemblance. He called it the Tasmanian wolf. In the 1872 edition of On the Origin of Species, he used the "close resemblance of the jaws of the dog and Tasmanian wolf" as an example of unrelated animals looking alike. Then he pointed out the giveaway. A dog has four premolars and two molars on each side of the upper jaw. The thylacine has three premolars and four molars. Count every tooth, and a thylacine has 46. An adult dog has 42.

Fact five. The animal that looked like a dog was closer kin to a kangaroo than to a dog. The thylacine and the dog have not shared a common ancestor since the Jurassic, the age of the dinosaurs, roughly 160 million years ago. The thylacine's closest living relatives are Tasmanian devils, quolls and their kin. So how did it end up with a dog's head? In 2018, a team led by Charles Feigin read its genome, its full set of DNA. They found the look-alike body was built mostly by changes in gene switches, the stretches of DNA that turn genes on and off. The same genome held another surprise. Its gene pool had already shrunk between about 70,000 and 120,000 years ago, before people reached Australia.

Fact six. It weighed about half the figure usually quoted. The commonly cited average is about thirty kilograms. In 2020, Douglass Rovinsky and his colleagues built 3D models from 93 adult thylacines held in museums and estimated their weight from the scans. The average came out at 16.7 kilograms. Males averaged about 19.7 kilograms, females about 13.7.

Fact seven. It was also called the Tasmanian wolf, but its skull matches a jackal's better than a wolf's. In 2021, Rovinsky's team compared the skulls of 56 carnivore species, looking for convergence, unrelated animals evolving the same shape. They found strong support for it with African jackals and the foxes of South America. With the gray wolf and the dingo, they found "no meaningful level of convergence." The team concluded the thylacine preferred relatively small prey, like bandicoots, young wallabies and small wallaby-like animals called pademelons. How it caught them is still argued about. In 2011, Borja Figueirido and Christine Janis studied its elbow and said it ambushed prey like a cat. A 2014 study comparing front legs called it "uniquely unspecialized," built to do a bit of everything. Then in 2026, an Australian team measured the way it walked on old zoo film. The team called it an opportunistic pouncer. In the words of the researcher Christofer Clemente, it was "similar to a fox, jackal or coyote."

Fact eight. London Zoo once sold a dead Tasmanian tiger to a taxidermist, someone who stuffs dead animals. When one female died of pneumonia in January 1906, the zoo sold her body for one pound and one shilling. About two dozen thylacines were shipped to the zoo, the first in 1850 and the last in 1926. The University of Tasmania counts five that arrived dead, and one more that died eight days after arriving. London never bred one. The last thylacine outside Australia died there in 1931.

Fact nine. Science first filed it as an opossum. In 1808, George Harris described it as Didelphis cynocephala, the "dog-headed opossum," and put it with the opossums of the Americas. It only got its own group name, Thylacinus, in 1824. Ordinary people never agreed on what to call it either. The Australian National Dictionary Centre lists eleven different names. Harris himself noted it was commonly called the "Zebra Opossum, Zebra Wolf." Others called it the marsupial wolf, the tiger wolf, the hyena, the hyena opossum and the dog-faced opossum.

Fact ten. Nobody ever recorded its voice. Every piece of film that survives is silent. The only record is written descriptions, and they don't agree. The Tasmanian government describes it as usually mute. When it was anxious or excited, it gave a series of husky, coughing barks. When it was hunting, it gave a terrier-like double yap, repeated every few seconds. Richard Lydekker's handbook to the marsupials, published in 1896, said something different: "Their cry appears to be limited to a dull guttural growl."

Fact eleven. All the film of living thylacines ever found adds up to about three minutes. That includes 21 seconds the National Film and Sound Archive released only in 2020, found inside a forgotten 1935 travel film. The archive says it all comes from fewer than a dozen source films, all black and white. In 2021, the archive released old thylacine footage in colour. The colourists had a problem. No one alive had seen its true colours, so they worked mainly from written descriptions of its coat, plus drawings and museum pelts. And at least one man paid for filming it. In December 1933, the naturalist David Fleay was bitten on the backside while he worked under his camera cloth. Before the thylacine bit him, it yawned at him. Twice.

That yawn is fact twelve. It is famous for that wide yawn, and you'll find claims online that it could open its mouth 120 degrees, or even 180. According to Guinness World Records, the skull's anatomy shows the jaw could not open wider than about 80 degrees. The widest-opening mouth of any mammal belongs to the hippo, at 150 degrees, nearly twice as wide.

Fact thirteen comes from a zoo you might not expect. A male thylacine lived at the National Zoological Park in Washington, D.C., until he died on January 11, 1905. The Smithsonian Archives still hold photographs of the zoo's thylacine pair, taken between 1902 and 1904. That male's brain was kept too. In 2017, Gregory Berns and Ken Ashwell noted that only four thylacine brains are documented to have survived intact. They scanned the Washington brain with a form of MRI that traces the brain's wiring, more than a century after the animal died. The results hinted that more of the brain's outer layer was given to planning than in a Tasmanian devil, which fits a predator, though the authors cautioned that the comparison rests on only two specimens.

Fact fourteen. The thylacine was the last of an entire family. Its family goes back at least 23 to 25 million years, to fossil beds at Riversleigh, where three new species were described in 2024. The smallest known member of the family weighed around 1.6 kilograms, smaller than a house cat. One big relative, Thylacinus potens, has been estimated at up to 55 kilograms. In August 2026, the first intact thigh bone of Thylacinus potens was announced from Alcoota, near Alice Springs. Adam Yates, a museum curator in the Northern Territory, said of it: "It doesn't look particularly large." It is about eight million years old, and it points to an animal of about 38 kilograms.

Fact fifteen. In the Swedish Museum of Natural History in Stockholm, there is a thylacine specimen about 130 years old. It had been dried and kept at room temperature. In 2023, a team led by Emilio Mármol-Sánchez extracted RNA from its skin and muscle. DNA is the full instruction manual. RNA shows which instructions a cell was actually using at the time. It's fragile, and no one had ever read which genes were active inside an extinct animal before. The team raised the number of known thylacine microRNAs, tiny molecules that help control genes, from 62 to 325. And they could see what its muscle cells had been doing, including genes for slow muscle fibres, the kind built for endurance, that were switched on when it died.

On the seventh of September 2026, it was ninety years since the last known thylacine died. A dried specimen in Stockholm, from an animal that died decades earlier, is still telling scientists which genes it was using. The next lost specimen could be on a wall somewhere, listed as a fox.
