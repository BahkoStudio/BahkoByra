Stand at the meat counter and read the labels. Beef, pork, chicken, lamb, turkey. Further down, venison and rabbit. Horse is not on that list. So why don't we eat horse meat?

The obvious guess is that it must be illegal. Across most of the world, horse is dinner. Kazakhstan serves it at weddings as a smoked sausage called kazy, washed down with fermented mare's milk. Many rural Kazakh families still slaughter a horse for the winter. The tradition has a name, sogym, and the whole family takes part. Japan serves it raw and thin-sliced under the name basashi, and the city of Kumamoto is famous for it. Towns in France and Belgium still have butcher shops that sell nothing else, marked with a golden horse head above the door. Italy puts it in stews and grinds it into salami. Mongolia and Switzerland eat it too, and Mexico slaughters horses for export. Sweden still sells smoked horse at the deli counter, under a name that never mentions the horse. Millions of horses are slaughtered for meat around the world every year. Even in the United States, eating horse remains legal under federal law. Britain never banned it either, and almost nobody there touches it. The slaughterhouses are a different story, and that part comes later.

The real answer starts with a letter. Pope Gregory III wrote it in 732 to a missionary named Boniface, who was working among the Germanic tribes north of the Alps. Boniface had already made his name by cutting down a sacred oak at Geismar, a tree the tribes believed belonged to the god Thor. The letter told him to stamp out one habit in particular. The tribes ate horses. They ate them at religious feasts, after sacrificing the animals to their gods. The horse feast sat close to the center of the old northern religion. A German chronicler writing in the eleventh century described horses hanging in a sacred grove beside the great temple at Uppsala in Sweden. To the church, horse meat was pagan worship on a plate, and the pope called it unclean. His letter covered wild horses and tame ones alike. Converting northern Europe meant getting the horse off the table. Missionaries carried that instruction across the continent for the next three centuries. Boniface himself never saw the end of it. A band of pagans killed him in what is now the Netherlands in 754.

Iceland shows how much the horse mattered. Around the year 1000, the island's assembly agreed to adopt Christianity, and the record of that decision survives. The new faith came with conditions attached. Icelanders could go on eating horse meat, and could go on sacrificing to the old gods as long as they did it in private. A whole nation negotiated its religion around a plate of horse. Within a few years those exceptions were dropped, and the taboo settled in there as well. It loosened again centuries later, and Icelanders eat horse today.

You can still hear that history in English. After the Normans conquered England in 1066, French-speaking nobles ate what English-speaking farmers raised. The farmer said cow. The lord said boeuf. English kept both, cow in the field and beef on the plate. Pig became pork and sheep became mutton. Even the deer got a dinner word, venison, from the French word for hunting. Veal came the same way, from the French veau. Horse never got a second word. Nobody at the top table was ordering it, so the language never needed one. Modern French has a word for horse meat, chevaline. English still has nothing.

The taboo landed hardest in the English-speaking world. In 1868 a group of reformers held a banquet at London's Langham Hotel to launch horse meat as cheap food for the poor. The menu ran from horse soup to roast horse. The guests were polite about it. Nobody came back for seconds, and the idea went nowhere.

The taboo did not stop Americans from killing horses for meat. It moved the killing out of sight. As late as 2006, the United States had three working horse slaughterhouses, two in Texas and one in Illinois. All three were owned by European companies, and the meat went to Belgium and Japan. Texas had a law from 1949 banning the sale of horse meat for people, and nobody had enforced it for half a century. A federal appeals court upheld that law in 2007, and the two Texas plants were finished. Congress had already cut the money for the federal inspectors, and without inspectors no plant could legally operate. Illinois banned horse slaughter that same year. All three plants were gone before 2008. The horses kept coming. Every year since, tens of thousands of American horses have been bought at auction and trucked to slaughterhouses in Mexico and Canada. The trade calls the men who buy them kill buyers, and a horse might change hands for a few hundred dollars. Racehorses that stopped winning turn up at those auctions, and so do children's ponies that were outgrown. Old horses and injured horses ride for many hours in the same trailer, next to horses somebody simply stopped paying for. The meat is packed on the far side of the border and shipped to the same customers as before.

There is a second problem with those horses, and it is a chemical one. Cattle and pigs are raised as food from birth, so every drug they receive is tracked and limited. Horses in America are raised as athletes and as pets. A racehorse with sore legs gets phenylbutazone, a painkiller so common that stable hands just call it bute. Bute is banned in any animal meant for food, in the United States and in Europe. No safe level for humans has been set. Under European rules, a single recorded dose makes a horse ineligible as food for the rest of its life. A horse that was never supposed to become food has no food record at all. Europe requires a signed statement that a slaughter horse has been drug-free for six months. Often there is no way to check it. The European Union stopped accepting horse meat from Mexico in 2015 over exactly this problem. Canada still ships it.

The horse is also older in America than the taboo. Horses evolved in North America and vanished from it around ten thousand years ago, at the end of the last ice age. The Spanish carried them back in the fifteen hundreds, and every mustang alive today descends from an animal that arrived by ship.

Those mustangs carry a price tag of their own. The federal government manages the wild herds, and when the range cannot feed them, it rounds them up. Selling them for slaughter is politically impossible. That leaves the government holding tens of thousands of horses in corrals and pastures. It pays to feed them for the rest of their lives, at a cost of tens of millions of dollars every year. It even pays people to adopt them. At times there have been more horses in government pens than running free on the range.

Food inspectors in Ireland announced the results of DNA tests in January 2013, run on frozen beef burgers from several supermarket chains, including Tesco. Some of the burgers contained horse, and one supermarket brand tested at nearly a third horse. Within weeks the same tests were running across Europe. Batches of Findus beef lasagne sold in Britain tested as mostly horse, and product after product came off the shelves. The meat had passed through traders in half a dozen countries, France and Romania among them, before it reached a British freezer. Tesco took out full-page newspaper apologies. Millions of people had been eating horse without knowing it. The health risk turned out to be small. Frozen burger sales in Britain still fell by close to half in the weeks that followed.

America watched from a distance and felt safe. The country has more horses than any other on Earth, several million of them, and almost none are raised to be eaten. California voters banned horse slaughter for human food back in 1998, and a few states followed. The inspection money came back in 2011, and a company in New Mexico got as far as a permit. Animal groups sued, and the plant never processed a single horse. Then the money was pulled again in 2014, and no plant has opened since. Every appropriations bill since has carried the same line. Most states still have no law about horse at all.

The meat has always been the easy part. It is leaner than beef, with more iron and a slightly sweet taste. Parts of America sold it in shops during the Second World War, when beef was rationed. Harvard's faculty club kept horse steak on the menu for decades after the war. It turned up again in a few supermarkets in 1973, when beef prices spiked. Both times, the moment prices came down, it vanished from the shelves.

What changed the horse was the job. For most of history it pulled the cart and carried the soldier. A worn-out horse was sold off like a worn-out ox. The engine took those jobs away within a single lifetime. The horses that stayed were the ones people rode for pleasure and kept as companions. Black Beauty, published in 1877 as a plea against cruelty to horses, became one of the best-selling books in the English language. American horses today have names and their own veterinarians. A horse that can no longer be ridden goes to a sanctuary or a retirement pasture. Hundreds of those exist across the country. The horse now sits in the same box as the dog. Congress made the dog and cat meat trade a federal crime in 2018.

A taboo is a decision somebody made for you a long time ago and never mentioned.

So read the labels again the next time you stand at the counter. Beef and pork. Then the gap where a word for horse was never invented.
