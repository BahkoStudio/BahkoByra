You are standing under a rock overhang in the far north of Australia, and there is an animal painted on the wall in front of you. It has a long body like a dog's, a heavy snout, and a long tail that tapers to a point. Across its body someone has added a pattern of fine white lines. Remember the white. Who painted it? When? What were they looking at? Had they ever seen one? Had anyone they knew? The animal is supposed to have died out here about three thousand years ago. Part of this painting is nowhere near that old. So did someone stand where you are standing and paint an animal that no living person had laid eyes on?

The animal is a thylacine, the one most people call the Tasmanian tiger. It was a marsupial, which means it carried its young in a pouch like a kangaroo, even though it looked like a dog. The last known thylacine died in a zoo in Hobart, Tasmania, on the seventh of September, 1936, fifty-nine days after the species was finally given legal protection. What fewer people know is that Tasmania was only the last place it lived. For most of its history the thylacine ranged across the Australian mainland too. More than 160 pictures of it have been found on rock across the continent, and the oldest are thought to be around fifteen thousand years old. The people who lived alongside it painted and engraved it again and again.

Then, on the mainland, the thylacine disappeared. The wall you are standing at is on that mainland. For a long time the date for the disappearance came from bones. In 2018 Lauren White and her colleagues at the University of Adelaide published a careful new version. They added twenty new radiocarbon dates from thylacine bones, the test that reads a bone's age from the carbon left in it, to the reliable dates already on record. They did the same for the Tasmanian devil. Both animals gave almost the same answer: gone from the mainland about 3,200 years ago, give or take a few decades.

There was a suspect waiting at the scene. Also in 2018, Jane Balme, Sue O'Connor and Stewart Fallon dated dingo bones from Madura Cave, on the Nullarbor Plain in the south. Their result put dingoes there by about the same time. A new predator shows up in the record, and at about the same moment the two native predators drop out of it. And there was one place the dingo never reached. Tasmania had been cut off from the mainland by rising seas roughly ten to twelve thousand years ago, long before any dingo arrived. There the thylacine and the devil hung on.

It is a tidy story, and it has still been argued over. Thomas Prowse and his colleagues at the University of Adelaide ran computer simulations of the three things that could have killed the mainland animals off: the dingo, a drying climate, and a growing human population. They found the dingo was unlikely to have done it alone. But that argument is about the cause. The date was the part that seemed settled.

That is what makes the wall in front of you a problem. So why was anyone still painting a thylacine? This wall is not the only one. In March 2026, a team from Griffith University published fourteen thylacine paintings that had never been recorded before, and two Tasmanian devils, in the journal Archaeology in Oceania. The team was led by Paul Taçon, a rock art researcher. With him were Andrea Jalandoni, also from Griffith, and Joey Nganjmirra, one of the Aboriginal people who hold country here. In partnership with Traditional Owners, the team had been working since 2018 at two places east of the East Alligator River, in western Arnhem Land: Awunbarna, which maps call Mount Borradaile, and Injalak Hill, beside the town of Gunbalanya. If you are wondering how anyone tells a painted thylacine from a painted dingo, you have already seen the features on your own wall: the long dog-like body, the tapering tail, the heavy snout, and, on some paintings, stripes and sharp teeth.

How do you put an age on a painting? It is harder than it sounds. The red and yellow paints are ochre, which is ground-up iron-rich rock, and it has no carbon in it, so the usual radiocarbon test has nothing to measure. In the Kimberley, in the north-west, Damien Finch and his colleagues at the University of Melbourne have used a way around that. Mud wasps build nests on rock walls, and the mud holds specks of charcoal, which can be dated. A nest built over a painting has to be younger than the painting. A nest the paint runs across has to be older. With the permission of the Traditional Owners they collected more than a hundred nests, and pinned one painted kangaroo to about seventeen thousand years old. For this painting, the clue was already in the paint itself.

Pigments do not age at the same speed. Charcoal goes first. The whites go next. Yellow ochre outlasts them, and deep red ochre usually outlasts everything, because it soaks into the rock and stains it. White sits on the surface of the rock and flakes off quickly. The rule of thumb the team gives is that most paintings with white in them are less than a thousand years old. Two of the newly recorded thylacines were painted with white clay as well as red. A third, in red and yellow, is the one in front of you: a pattern of fine crossed white lines was added over its body within the past few hundred years. "White doesn't last very long," Taçon said, "so they're younger than we would expect."

Look at the wall again with that in mind. The red lines are the older part, soaked into the stone. The white is the part that should not be there. Somebody laid that white over the animal, in this shelter, long after the last thylacine on the mainland is supposed to have died.

Taçon offered two explanations, and he was careful to offer both. The first is the obvious one. "The artists who made the more recent paintings may have seen actual living thylacines," he said, "and some of these creatures may have survived longer in Arnhem Land." The second is quieter. The artists may have been inspired by earlier paintings. Asked which it was, he gave the honest answer: "We can't exactly tell."

Take the first one seriously, because it is harder to dismiss than it sounds. A date like 3,200 years ago is an estimate built from the bones that happened to survive, in the places people happened to dig. It tells you when the record stops. It cannot tell you when the last animal died.

And we have watched that gap open up in our own time, with this same species. The International Union for Conservation of Nature did not declare the thylacine extinct until 1982, and the Tasmanian government waited until 1986, because the rule then was fifty years with no confirmed evidence since the last captive animal died. That same year, 1982, a wildlife ranger named Hans Naarding reported watching one in his spotlight for about three minutes in north-west Tasmania. The state wildlife service searched the area for more than a year and found nothing to confirm it. In 2023 Barry Brook and his colleagues at the University of Tasmania went through 1,237 records from Tasmania, going back to 1910, and weighted each one by how believable it was. They put the chance that the thylacine is alive today at less than one percent, but their best estimate is that it lasted in the Tasmanian bush until somewhere between the late 1980s and the early 2000s. Half a century is far short of two thousand years, but it is the same kind of mistake. If we may have lost track of an animal we had photographed, then a small population hanging on in the stone country of Arnhem Land, long after the bones run out, is hard to rule out.

But the same country holds a warning about reading too much into a picture. In 2010 the archaeologist Robert Gunn looked at a red painting of a huge bird on Jawoyn country, in south-west Arnhem Land. It reminded him of Genyornis, a giant bird that died out more than forty thousand years ago, and if he was right, and the painter had seen one alive, it was the oldest painting on Earth. In 2017 a team led by the archaeologist Bryce Barker dated the rock itself. The painted surface had only been exposed when an overhang collapsed, a little under fourteen thousand years ago. The painting could not be older than the wall. A likeness had been read as an eyewitness. That is why Taçon will not say more than the paint allows.

Taçon's second explanation is stranger, and it starts back at the wall. In northern Australia a rock painting can go on being painted long after the first painter walks away. Images that matter get renewed. The Kimberley elder David Mowaljarlai and his co-authors described the practice in the journal Antiquity back in 1988, and Andrea Jalandoni saw the signs of it at these sites. Some of the paintings had been retouched, she said, which showed how much these animals meant across generations. So the white lines on the wall in front of you may be a repair, laid over a picture far older.

And the animal still has a name. In western Arnhem Land the thylacine is called djankerrk. In the stories, the Rainbow Serpent kept two of them as pets. Joey Nganjmirra put it plainly: "They used to tell stories about going hunting with thylacines." Other stories tie the animal to rock pools and to swimming. How long can a story like that last? In 2016 the geographer Patrick Nunn and the linguist Nicholas Reid collected twenty-one Aboriginal stories from around the coast that describe the sea rising and drowning the land, and matched each one to the shoreline it described. One story described a coastline drowned so long ago that when it was checked against sea-level reconstructions, the date came out at over seven thousand years.

Next to that, holding on to an animal for two thousand years is a small thing. So why did someone paint an animal that was already extinct? Maybe it was not extinct, and the accepted date is out by two thousand years, at least in Arnhem Land. Or maybe it was, and people went on painting it anyway, from older pictures and from stories, because it mattered too much to leave off the wall. Nobody can say which from the paint alone. But notice which word in the question is no longer safe under either answer. It is "already". If the animal lived on, nobody watched the last mainland thylacine die, and every extinction date you have ever read is built on the last time somebody found something. If it lived on only in paint and story, then for the people in this shelter it had not gone anywhere. Within the last thousand years, in that shelter, the thylacine was still present to someone.

Some artists in western Arnhem Land are still painting djankerrk, the thylacine, on bark, paper and canvas. The white on the wall in front of you is flaking. Give it a few hundred years and it will likely be gone, the way white goes, and the red underneath will look as old as everything around it. Unless someone comes back with fresh paint.

There is another plain question on this channel with an awkward answer. It is called Why You Can't Buy Horse Meat in the US.
