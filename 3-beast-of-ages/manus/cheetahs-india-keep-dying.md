## Del 2: Manuset

*2 044 ord, cirka 14 minuter. Snittmening 15,0 ord, längsta 31, noll tankstreck. Skrivs och läses på engelska. CTA:n ingår som sista rad.*

On the 21st of September 2026, a cheetah named Gaurav died in India's Kuno National Park. He was one of the first eight cheetahs India flew in from Namibia four years ago, to bring the species back to the country. Six of those eight are now dead.

Three days before Gaurav died, a young female born in India gave birth to four cubs. India's environment minister posted about it the same day: "Four years, four new lives." It was her second litter of 2026.

Both are true. Cheetahs really are back in India, for the first time in more than 70 years. And they really do keep dying. In January 2022, before the first one landed, India published an action plan that spelled out what failure would look like after five years. Counting from the first landing, those five years run out on the 17th of September 2027.

The last three wild cheetahs anyone ever confirmed in India were shot in 1947. All three were shot by the maharaja, or king, of a small Indian state that happened to be called Korea. His private secretary sent a photograph of the bodies to the Journal of the Bombay Natural History Society. In 1952, the government declared the species extinct in the country.

India first tried to bring cheetahs back in the 1970s, offering to trade Asiatic lions for Asiatic cheetahs from Iran, and the talks fell apart. In the early 2000s, India asked Iran again, and Iran said no, even to letting Indian scientists clone its cheetahs. Finally, in January 2020, India's Supreme Court allowed cheetahs from Africa to be brought in "on an experimental basis."

So the cheetahs in India today are African cheetahs. They're the same species as the Asiatic cheetah that once lived here, but a different branch of the family tree. A 2020 study in the journal Scientific Reports estimated that India's old cheetahs and those of southeastern Africa split around 72,000 years ago. Iran has fewer than 30 Asiatic cheetahs left in the wild, and India's action plan says taking any would hurt their survival.

The first eight cheetahs landed on the 17th of September 2022, on a modified Boeing 747. It was Prime Minister Narendra Modi's 72nd birthday. In Namibia, one of them had been called Elton. In India, he became Gaurav. Twelve more came from South Africa in February 2023, and nine from Botswana in February 2026. That's 29 cheetahs flown in from Africa.

Ten of those 29 are now dead. Add the cubs born in India, and at least two dozen cheetahs have died since 2022, depending on who's counting.

The first to go was Sasha, from Namibia, in March 2023. She died of kidney failure, an illness she already had before she arrived. A male called Uday died of heart and lung failure in April. A female called Daksha died in May, after a male injured her during a mating attempt.

The cubs had it hard too. In March 2023, a Namibian female called Jwala gave birth to four cubs, the first cheetahs born in India in more than 70 years. In May, three of them died of weakness and dehydration. One died on the 23rd of May, the hottest day of that summer. It reached around 46 or 47 degrees Celsius, about 115 Fahrenheit. The one survivor, a female later named Mukhi, was raised by hand.

Then, in the monsoon of 2023, some of the cheetahs started growing thick winter coats. In southern Africa, June is midwinter, and their bodies still ran on that clock. But India's monsoon is the wettest part of the year. Under that heavy, soaking fur, the radio collars around their necks kept the skin constantly wet. Flies laid eggs in the damp skin. The eggs hatched into maggots, and the wounds they made turned into infections that spread through the blood.

A male called Tejas died on the 11th of July with wounds on his neck. The official post-mortem, the medical exam after death, said "traumatic shock," meaning shock from his injuries. Three days later, a male called Suraj died of that blood infection, septicemia. Adrian Tordiffe, a wildlife vet from the University of Pretoria who advised the project, blamed the collars. He later said he knew what was killing them the moment he saw video of Suraj, before any post-mortem.

That month, Tordiffe and three other South African experts, including the cheetah specialist Vincent van der Merwe, wrote a letter to India's Supreme Court. They said they had become "mere window dressing," there for show, with no real say. The judges didn't hold back either. "Why are you making it a prestige issue?" they asked the government, meaning a matter of national pride. Forty percent of the cheetahs dying in less than a year, they said, "does not present a good picture." In August, a Namibian female called Dhatri was found dead in the forest, with maggots in her wounds.

One of the project's own advisers had predicted worse. Van der Merwe said the deaths were expected: "We anticipate a 50 per cent mortality in the first year." South Africa, he pointed out, had needed 26 years to learn how to move cheetahs into new reserves, and had lost 279 cheetahs along the way. In June 2025, government scientists writing in the journal Frontiers in Conservation Science reported that 70 percent of the adult cheetahs flown in had survived their first year in India. The court's 40 percent had counted three dead cubs too. The action plan had aimed for at least half.

So the deaths alone don't settle it. The bigger problem is space.

That action plan estimated that Kuno National Park, which covers 748 square kilometers, about 290 square miles, could hold "a maximum of 21 cheetahs."

Today, Kuno holds 51.

Most of them live behind fences. On the project's fourth anniversary, only 17 of the cheetahs at Kuno were free in the open forest.

The plan's numbers assumed about three cheetahs could share every 100 square kilometers. In 2022, a team led by the ecologist Arjun Gopalaswamy published a critique in the journal Nature Ecology and Evolution. They pointed out that cheetahs roaming free in Africa usually live at fewer than one per 100 square kilometers. In other words, the plan had counted on at least three times as many cheetahs sharing the same ground. The cheetahs themselves seem to agree with the critics. In December 2025, the government reported that a South African female called Gamini and her four nearly grown cubs were using an area of 6,160 square kilometers. That's more than eight times the size of the park.

When cheetahs outgrow a park, they leave it. That same month, one of Gamini's sons, about 20 months old, strayed out of the park and was killed by a vehicle on the Agra to Mumbai highway. In April 2026, a young male born to Aasha, one of the original Namibian females, walked more than 150 kilometers into Ranthambore, a tiger reserve in the neighboring state of Rajasthan. After about three weeks there, rangers knocked him out with a tranquilizer dart and brought him back. And in June, one of Gamini's daughters was found badly injured in a forest outside the park, and died.

Darting has become routine at Kuno. In 2024, the chief wildlife warden of Madhya Pradesh, the state Kuno is in, inspected the project. He found that in its first two years, cheetahs had been knocked out with tranquilizer drugs 110 times. The government scientists' 2025 paper counted fewer, 90 times in two and a half years, with no deaths from the drugs.

Kuno, the park they keep getting carried back to, already has a top predator. The action plan called conflict with leopards "a natural process," but said the leopard population would need to be "managed" in the first four to five years. In May 2026, an official told The Tribune that Kuno and the buffer zone around it held more than 150 leopards.

That same spring, the young female from the start of this story gave birth to four cubs. She's known only by a code, KGP12, and she's another of Gamini's daughters. And these were the first cubs born in the wild, outside any enclosure. Their den lay beyond the park's protected forest. On the 12th of May, all four were found dead near the den, partly eaten. The park's field director said a leopard was the most likely killer.

The critics have been blunt. Ravi Chellam, a wildlife scientist and longtime critic of the project, called it "a glorified safari" in 2024. Ullas Karanth, one of India's best-known tiger scientists, told National Geographic in 2023 that the idea of a self-sustaining population was "completely absurd." He meant cheetahs that keep their numbers up on their own, with no new ones flown in. Gus Mills, a South African carnivore expert, said in 2025 that "Kuno could never contain a viable population," one big enough to last.

Two people who know the project from the inside see it differently. Yadvendradev Jhala led the science behind it until he was removed in 2023, and he was sharply critical of how it was run. Yet he said in June 2026 that the project "is on the right track." And Laurie Marker founded the Cheetah Conservation Fund in Namibia and helped move the first eight cheetahs. She wrote in April 2026 that with fewer than 7,000 cheetahs left in the wild, "Standing still is often the greater risk."

The government is pressing ahead. It's aiming for 60 to 70 cheetahs across about 17,000 square kilometers by 2032, more than 20 times the size of Kuno. India now has 55 cheetahs, four of them at a second site called Gandhi Sagar. By April 2025, the project had cost more than 1.1 billion rupees, roughly 13 million dollars.

Across four years, cheetahs have given birth to more than 50 cubs in India. Thirty-six are alive, but only 13 of them are older than one year. Most of the young ones were born and raised behind fences. In November 2025, Mukhi, the hand-raised survivor of Jwala's litter, had cubs of her own, the first India-born cheetah to become a mother.

On the 18th of September, KGP12 gave birth again, four months after losing her first litter. Those are the minister's "four new lives." Reports disagree about whether she had them in the wild or inside an enclosure.

Three days after those cubs were born, Gaurav died. He was about ten years old. When he was rescued from the forest earlier that month, forest officials described him as very weak, his muscles wasting away, severely dehydrated. The post-mortem results haven't been released, and Chellam has asked for the report to be made public. Of the eight cheetahs that stepped off that plane in 2022, two are left: Aasha and Jwala.

So who's right? The action plan sets its own test. It counts the project a failure if the cheetahs "do not survive or fail to reproduce in five years." By that wording, the project has already passed. Enough of them have survived, and they have bred. The same section names a second danger: failing to secure "cheetah habitats in the larger landscape," meaning safe land outside the park for cheetahs to spread into. For now, cheetahs that wander out of Kuno are darted and carried back. And the plan's goals for success ask for more: cheetahs that "successfully reproduce in the wild," and wild-born cubs that survive "to more than 1 year."

So far, no cub born in the wild in India has been reported reaching its first birthday. The only confirmed wild litter died at about a month old, most likely killed by a leopard. Even if KGP12's new cubs were born in the wild, and even if they live, they will turn one on the 18th of September 2027. That's the day after the plan's five-year mark.
